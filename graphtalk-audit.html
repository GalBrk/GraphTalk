What the audit found, in plain language

A review of every piece of statistical work by Nitzan Zacharia and Inbal Moryles in the GraphTalk repository. This page explains each problem without statistics jargon: what the code does, why it is wrong, and what it costs the project.

Method: 16 independent audit lenses, then adversarial verification of every finding by two more agents whose job was to disprove it, then three critics including one instructed to defend the students' work. 98 agents, roughly 8 million tokens, about four hours. Findings that could not be reproduced were thrown out and are listed near the bottom.

The short answer

The numbers are real, the reasoning around them is not always. Nothing is fabricated. Three of the main committed data files rebuild byte-for-byte from the current code, nothing is silently dropped from the pipeline, and only 2 rows out of 10,080 fail to parse. That is a genuinely good foundation, and better than most course projects.

But there is one real statistical error that removes one of the two headline results — the code miscounts how much independent data it has — and both surviving results are decided by a random seed rather than by the data. Separately, the sample-size calculator is wrong by a factor of 64-100, and it is about to be used to book a GPU run that isn't needed.

On your specific worry: "measuring partial information" — mostly no. Rows are not being quietly discarded; the pipeline uses all 180 pairs in every cell. "Cherry picking" — not in the sense of hiding data, but yes in the sense of selective reporting: a full replication run exists in the repo and only the one cell that agrees with the hypothesis is written up.

The most likely thing to embarrass you in a write-up is not the statistics — it is the prose. The documentation has drifted badly behind the data. The headline accuracy table is stale in 14 of 16 cells, and one number under a conclusion has the wrong sign.

Problems that change a result

These six are the ones that matter for what the project can claim. Each was confirmed by an agent whose instructions were to refute it.

High Counting the same graph six times 1

What the code does

You have 30 graphs. You ask 6 different questions about each one (how many nodes? how many edges? what is node 3's degree? and so on). That is 180 question-answers per model.

A statistics test needs to know how many independent pieces of evidence you have. Independent means: knowing one does not help you guess another. The code told the test it had 180 independent items. It actually has 30 — because all 6 questions are about the same graph, with the same primer text attached. If a graph is easy, the model tends to get all 6 right; if it is awkward, it tends to struggle on several. They move together.

Think of it like this. You survey 30 households and ask 6 people in each household who they will vote for. You then tell your statistics software you have 180 independent voters. You do not — you have 30 households. Family members agree with each other, so you have far less information than the count suggests. Your error bars come out too narrow and results look more significant than they really are.

Why it happened

A row's ID looks like node_count/7 — the task name plus the graph number. The code grouped by that whole string. But graph 7 in the node_count task is literally the same graph as graph 7 in the edge_count task; verified from prompts.jsonl, the text is byte-identical. So one graph was treated as six.

So this choice is exactly what created both of the project's significant results, while the documentation says it changed nothing. Two other places describe the count as "real graph instances", which it is not.

A curious side effect: because the task name is inside the ID, every group currently has exactly one member — so the whole clustering machinery does literally nothing on the committed data. It only starts working once the key is fixed.

High Both surviving results are decided by a random seed 2

What the code does

The p-value here is not calculated from a formula. It is estimated by shuffling the data 10,000 times at random and counting how often the shuffled version looks as extreme as the real one. That means the answer can only ever land on a multiple of 1/10,001 — steps of about 0.0001.

The pass/fail lines the project uses are 0.0005 and 0.001. Those are only 5 and 10 steps up from zero. So the verdicts land on the measurement grid, decided by a handful of individual random draws.

Think of it like this. You have a ruler marked only in centimetres, and you need to decide whether something is over or under 5 mm. Sometimes it will read 0 cm and sometimes 1 cm for the same object, depending on how you happen to hold it. That is not a measurement of the object; it is a measurement of how you held the ruler.
What it costs
margin of the project's only fully-corrected result0.00000005it clears the bar by 5 hundred-millionths
the measurement's own resolution0.00012,000× coarser than that margin

The second case is worse, because it was written up as a finding. analysis/README.md describes one cell moving from "survives correction" to "does not" as a "Real effect on the current data" caused by a methodology change. It is one hit out of 10,001 (10 versus 11), and it reverses under 16 of 18 other random seeds:

Same data, same code, only the random seed changed — 18 seeds tested
Filled = the 16 seeds that give the opposite verdict to the one published. Outlined = the 2 that agree with it. Raising the shuffle count to 200,000 with the committed seed also gives the opposite answer.

Neither results document mentions the seed or the resolution anywhere — the word "seed" appears 0 times in 697 lines of analysis/README.md.

Fix: raise the shuffle count (200,000 is cheap here), record the seed and the count in the emitted report, and have the script flag any cell whose p-value sits within noise of its threshold. Then delete the "Real effect on the current data" sentence — that cell is simply on the boundary and cannot be resolved at the current setting.

High The sample-size calculator is wrong by 64-100× 3

What the code does

There is a script that answers a planning question: "this result was not significant — how many more graphs would I need to collect?" It gets the answer wrong, in one direction only, by about 80×.

The cause is that two different quantities are both called "the effect size", and they are in different units:

  • Quantity A — "push each wrong answer's chance of becoming right up by 40 percentage points."
  • Quantity B — "overall accuracy went up by 4.4 percentage points."

These are not the same scale. If the model is already 89% correct, only 11% of its answers can flip. So a 40-point push applied to that 11% produces just 0.40 × 0.11 = 4.4 points of overall movement. The simulator returns A. The report column is B. The script divides A by B and squares the result.

Think of it like this. Your instrument reports a distance as "40" in kilometres and "4.4" in miles. You divide them, get 9, square it, and conclude you need to travel 81 times further. You actually need to travel about the same distance. The units were never comparable.
Why the direction matters

The squashing factor is "how much room is left to improve". Near the top of the scale there is almost no room, so improvements get inflated enormously. Near the bottom there is lots of room to fall, so harms barely get inflated at all. The result:

"primer helps" questions64-100×inflated, so all look unaffordable
"primer hurts" questions1.1-1.3×roughly correct, so all look cheap

So the conclusion "we would need a larger or restructured corpus" for every helps-hypothesis is not a finding about your data. It is an artifact of this bug.

→
actually needed~30 graphsalready have 30; power is ~90%

The cell in question (qwen3-8b/degree under GoT naming) already has p = 0.0018 with the 30 graphs you have. You are about to spend cluster time confirming something you already have.

Why the safety check missed it

validate_recommend_count.py skips any cell needing more than 10,000 units. Every affected cell needs at least 14,580. So the validation that reports the method is "conservative in every cell checked" only ever checked the direction that is not broken.

Fix: compare like with like — use the simulator's own mde_realized_diff column, which is already on the same scale as delta. Then re-run and re-size the follow-up (it may not be needed at all). Raise the validator's cap so it covers the affected cells.

High The power simulator can only ever simulate improvement, never harm 4

What the code does

To answer "how big an effect could I have detected with this much data?", the code invents a fake effect of a known size, injects it into the data, and checks whether its own test would find it. That is a good idea. But the way it injects the fake effect, answers can only flip from wrong to right. Never right to wrong.

Real data flips both ways — in one pooled cell, 10 answers improve and 21 get worse. A fake effect that only ever pushes one way is much easier to detect than a real one of the same size, so the test looks more sensitive than it is.

Think of it like this. You are testing whether a scale can detect a 1 kg change. You test it by only ever adding weight, in a room with no vibration. It passes. In the real world weight goes both up and down and the room shakes, and the same scale misses the change most of the time. You measured the easy case and reported it as the general one.
What it costs
claimed detection power80%at the published effect sizes
→
actual, re-simulated properly20-33%across four cells checked

Every "we could have detected an effect this size" number in the reports is optimistic. Five committed rows are internally contradictory as a result: they claim 80% power for an effect they also report as not detected. And the docstring specifically claims the design prevents this exact problem, which it does not.

This sits underneath problem 3 and is independent of it: even the quantity problem 3 treats as "the correct scale" is itself too small by 2-4×.

Fix: inject the fake effect so it produces flips in both directions, calibrated to each cell's own observed rate of disagreement. The verifier wrote out the exact replacement formula; mirror it in validate_recommend_count.py too, and add one test that checks the simulator's power estimate is honest.

What is wrong

The section headed "A third pass" in analysis/README.md (lines 131-198) says "Confirmed on the current data" and "Confirmed on the real regenerated data". Every number in it reproduces only against a superseded version of the report.

Medium A full replication run exists, and only the agreeing cell was reported 7

What is there

The Game-of-Thrones naming run is not a side experiment. It re-tests the same 100 hypotheses on the same 180 graphs with the same correct answers, with only the node names changed. That makes it a replication. It found 7 cells significant within their family. The write-up reports 1 — the one that supports "primers help".

What the same committed file also says, unmentioned anywhere
  • Pooled rwse — the integer run's other flagged headline (p = 0.0105) — measures exactly 0.000, p = 1.000 in the replication. A flat non-replication of a headline cell, on the same graphs.
  • The same model and the same condition being promoted also significantly increases timeouts in the thinking arm (0 out of 180 to 7 out of 180, p = 0.014). That is a cost, in the unfavourable direction, for the thing being recommended.
  • Correcting the two runs as one family of tests, which is what they are, leaves nothing significant.
Think of it like this. You run your experiment twice. The second run agrees on one result and flatly disagrees on the other. Writing up only the agreement is not fraud — the second run is right there in the repo — but a reader who does not open the CSV gets a much stronger impression than the evidence supports.
In fairness

A verifier checked whether the two rwse estimates genuinely conflict and found they are statistically compatible (the naming-by-condition interaction is p = 0.085). So the replication neither confirms nor refutes that cell — which is exactly what should have been written down. This is a completeness gap, not a false claim.

Fix: three sentences in the GoT subsection. State that degree replicates and rwse does not, that the difference between runs is itself not significant so neither cell is settled, and that the same condition raises timeouts.

Medium The "independent confirmation" is the same calculation, and the one that disagreed was deleted 8

What is claimed

analysis/README.md says a result is "corroborated by an independent GEE fit (p = 0.0007)". A GEE is a second, different statistical model, so this reads as independent confirmation.

What is actually true

The second model was deliberately set up so its answer equals the first model's answer. Checked across all 48 cells, the two agree to 14 decimal places. Its p-value is smaller than the first test's in 48 out of 48 cells. So agreement is guaranteed by construction, and the "confirmation" adds no evidence — it just re-tests the same number with a more permissive test.

Think of it like this. You weigh a parcel, then confirm the weight by weighing it again on the same scale with a magnifying glass on the dial. You have not independently confirmed anything, and the magnifying glass makes you more confident, not more correct.
Three further problems
  • The stated reason for not correcting the second model for multiple comparisons — "the joint fit already adjusts each condition for the others" — is arithmetically false. Refitting each condition on its own reproduces the joint fit to 13 decimal places. So these are six separate tests, and uncorrected: 0 of them survive the project's own correction.
  • Half the cited claim has no supporting file at all — the pooled result it says was corroborated does not exist in the GEE output.
  • There is one cell where the second model disagrees with the first. It is reported nowhere, and its only written record was in a scratch log deleted under an unrelated commit message ("fix the truncated_keys node_naming key gap").
Fix: replace the false rationale in the docstring with the true one, apply the project's own correction to the GEE output before citing it, drop the word "independent", and either report the disagreeing cell or say why not.

Medium Of three ways to handle cut-off answers, the one kept is the only one that produced a result 9

The situation

Some model answers never finish — they hit the token limit and stop mid-sentence (348 rows). There are three defensible ways to handle them, and at one point the code reported all three side by side:

OptionSignificant main-sweep results
(a) throw them awaynone (p = 0.0110 / 0.0757)
(b) count them all as correctnone (p = 0.0125 / 0.1406)
(c) count them all as wrongtwo (p = 0.0065 / 0.0118)

The code was then changed to do only (c), and (a) and (b) were deleted from the report so a reader can no longer compare. Both of the project's main-sweep accuracy findings exist only under (c).

Why this is ranked medium, not high

The audit's defence critic pushed back here and was right. Option (c) is genuinely the most defensible of the three: a cut-off answer that happens to contain the right number should not count as the model getting it right. It is also the harsher choice — it makes "primers help" harder to claim, not easier. And the README does say the results are "new since Phase 2" and does discuss the direction of the bias with a measured magnitude.

So this is a missing disclosure, not data selection. What no file says is that the retained option was the only one of three previously-published options that produced any result.

Fix: one sentence — "these two cells were significant under only one of the three bounds previously reported; under the excluded and best-case bounds neither reaches significance."

Low The "pre-registration" was written after the result it registers 11

The background

Pre-registration means writing down which hypothesis you are testing before you look at the data. It is what stops you finding a pattern by luck and then claiming you predicted it. It also earns you an easier significance threshold, because you are making fewer claims.

The timeline
15:23, 4 Septresultreport identifies qwen3-8b/degree as the significant cell
→
18:32, same day+3h09m"pre-registration" file names exactly that cell

analysis/README.md describes the mechanism as naming cells "decided before a sweep's results were seen".

Why this ends up low, not high

The verifiers checked and softened this, correctly. The file is scoped to a future run that has not happened yet, and it is applied to no committed result — the relevant column is empty in every shipped file. Pre-registering a cell for a genuine future replication is normal, good practice. So no published number is corrupted.

What survives is a wording problem with real teeth: the code and docs both call a one-cell pre-registered family "small, strict". A family with one member is tested at the plain 0.05 threshold while the other 99 cells face 0.000505 — about 99× more permissive. That is the opposite of strict, and nothing says so.

Fix: correct the "strict" wording, and add a field to the config recording which dataset it was written for, so a pre-registration can never be silently applied to the data it was chosen from.

Medium Some "95% confidence intervals" are really 87%, and six of them are zero width 14

The confidence intervals are built by resampling. On cells where almost nothing disagrees — as little as 2 out of 180 pairs — that method breaks down. Measured true coverage on those rows is 86.8%, not 95%, and the miss is entirely on one side: the harm side, which is exactly the side the README reads as a safety bound.

17 of 60 rows are in this regime, including all six cells for one model. Six rows publish a zero-width "95% CI" of [0.000, 0.000], which is not an interval at all.

Think of it like this. A survey where only 2 of 180 people expressed any preference cannot support a 95% confidence interval — resampling those 2 people over and over just returns the same 2 opinions. The interval it prints is a description of the resampling, not of the population.
Fix: when there are too few disagreeing pairs, print no interval (or an exact binomial one) instead of a percentile interval, and add a coverage test to the suite.
Where: graphtalk/significance.py:204-206 Whose: both

Medium All 12 tests for the cross-check pass with the cross-check switched off 15

The GEE module exists for exactly one reason: to account for the fact that answers about the same graph are related. The audit deleted that accounting from the code and all 12 of its tests still passed.

The reason is that every test fixture generates each row independently, so there is no relatedness for the code to account for. And the one test that looks like it verifies the grouping reads the number from pandas rather than from the fitted model — so it tests pandas, not the model.

Think of it like this. A smoke detector with a test that only ever checks the battery light. It will pass forever, including after someone removes the sensor.

No committed number is wrong because of this. The risk is that a future change silently breaks the cross-check while the test suite stays green.

Fix: read the group count off the fit rather than from pandas, and add one fixture with genuine within-graph correlation that fails if the grouping is removed.

What is genuinely good

Included so you can tell signal from noise. Several of these are things a reviewer would specifically look for, and they hold up.

  • Three major data files rebuild byte-for-byte from the current code: sweep_frame.csv, sweep_frame.got.csv and significance_report.csv. Nothing is hand-edited or fabricated; the pipeline is deterministic. This is the single most important thing to be able to say, and you can say it.
  • Nothing is silently dropped. Every main-sweep cell uses all 180 pairs, every pooled cell all 720. The audit specifically looked for exclusion rates that differ by condition and found none. Your "measuring partial information" worry does not hold in that form.
  • Only 2 rows out of 10,080 fail to parse. The extraction work, whatever else is true of how it was developed, works.
  • "degree helps" is robust. It survived every alternative grouping the audit could construct and got stronger, and its error bars were already conservative. Your central claim is the one that holds.
  • The Benjamini-Hochberg implementation is correct, checked against the textbook procedure, as are the resampling units for the permutation test and the bootstrap.
  • The MAE imputation cannot favour a condition — it uses a per-task constant computed across all conditions. This was specifically suspected and cleared.
  • The filler-condition instrument asymmetry is empirically harmless. The document argues it badly, but the conclusion is right: no row anywhere sits close enough to its token budget for the asymmetry to bite.
  • The unvalidated length-outlier heuristic feeds nothing. It is a diagnostic column only, so its 81 extra flagged rows never enter a reported number.
  • The repository documents its own limitations unusually thoroughly, including explicit retractions. That is why this audit could go as deep as it did — most of the raw material came from the authors' own notes.

Claims the audit itself got wrong

Each finding was reviewed by agents whose instructions were to disprove it. Eight claims did not survive. They are listed because an audit that only reports hits is not trustworthy — and because if any of these reach the group as criticism, they are wrong.

  • "The confidence intervals collapse to 59.7% coverage." False. A 2,000-run simulation gave 98.6% and 96.1% — slightly over-covered. Only the very sparsest one-sided case is degraded (86.8%), which is what problem 14 reports.
  • "Fixing the grouping also kills the degree result." False, and this was the audit's own headline claim initially. Degree survives every respecification.
  • "The bias diagnostics can never fire." False — the threshold is adjustable and the underlying count is reported on every row.
  • "The thinking arm's 5,040 rows get no accuracy analysis, hiding the largest effects." Refuted — the missing cells were computed and all 24 are null.
  • "The MAE imputation flips four effect signs." Refuted; the sign convention was read backwards.
  • "The Bayesian model's shared instance effect is indefensible." Refuted by all verifiers.
  • "The edge-existence extraction fallback can only ever help accuracy." Refuted.
  • "Including the omitted comparisons can only destroy significance." Measured, and the opposite is true — adding them yields 3 survivors, not 0.

What to do, in order

Must fix before anything is written up or submitted

  • Fix the grouping key (problem 1) and re-run. Then find out which results actually survive. Everything downstream depends on this answer.
  • Raise the shuffle count and record the seed (problem 2). Right now two published verdicts are coin flips, and one of them is written up as a substantive finding.
  • Regenerate the headline accuracy table and the four derived figures around it (problem 5) — including the filler number whose sign is wrong. Cheapest high-value fix on the list.
  • Fix the sample-size formula before booking any GPU time (problems 3 and 4). The planned 500-graph run is probably unnecessary.
  • Regenerate or retract "A third pass" (problem 6). It tells readers how to interpret everything else and its bound is 3× too tight.

Should fix cheap, and each one closes a real gap

  • Report the replication run honestly — degree replicates, rwse does not, neither is settled (problem 7).
  • Stop calling the GEE "independent", correct its false rationale, and apply the correction before citing it (problem 8).
  • One sentence disclosing that the two pooled results held under only one of three previously reported treatments (problem 9).
  • Point the four validation scripts at the same rows the pipeline uses (problem 13), and re-run.
  • Suppress confidence intervals where there are too few disagreeing pairs (problem 14).
  • Regenerate or stamp the stale artifacts, and fix the row counts in docs/DATA.md.

Say more clearly no re-analysis needed

  • Correct "small, strict" for the one-cell pre-registered family, and record which dataset a pre-registration was written for (problem 11).
  • Replace "can no longer be reproduced" with the command that reproduces it and what it shows (problem 10).
  • Add a test that fails when the cross-check's grouping is removed (problem 15).

Worth saying out loud a note on how to read this

Almost every problem on this page is a documentation-drift problem, not a competence problem. The pattern is consistent and recognisable: the data was regenerated many times, the code was fixed repeatedly and correctly, and the prose was not always regenerated alongside it. Two of the findings — the grouping key and the sample-size formula — are genuine technical errors of a kind that is easy to make and hard to see. Neither suggests anyone was trying to reach a particular answer.

The one place to be careful is the replication run, because the shape there — a second full pass exists, only the agreeing cell is written up — is the shape a reviewer is trained to look for, and it is fixable with three sentences. Fix it before someone else notices it.
