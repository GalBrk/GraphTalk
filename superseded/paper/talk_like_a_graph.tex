\begin{abstract}
Prepending a \emph{primer} of factual sentences about a graph's structure
(node degree, clustering coefficient, random-walk return probabilities or
component count) to its text encoding is a natural way to assist a
language model on graph-reasoning questions. An apparent gain is
uninformative if the answer can be read from the primer without consulting
the graph, and confounded if the primer merely lengthens the prompt. We
evaluate primers against four controls jointly: a graph-blind shortcut
solver refit at the evaluated graph size, a length-matched content-free
filler, a minimal-length content control, and a degree-preserving rewiring
that varies triangle count at fixed prompt length. Across four Qwen3 arms
(1.7B and 4B, with and without thinking mode) on $40$-node
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs, primers that state the answer help a model at
floor and harm a model at ceiling (cross-fitted interaction with baseline
accuracy $-11.0$ points, $95\%$ CI $[-17.1, -5.7]$), whereas primers that
do not state it show no such relation. The pattern replicates on eleven
held-out arms ($0.6$--$14$B, two families) but is not graded at
intermediate baselines. The one primer that does not state the answer and
still beats both controls, \texttt{clustering} on \texttt{node\_degree},
gains $+5.9$ points at intermediate densities and little elsewhere; two
candidate mechanisms for it are not supported.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph} show that a large language model's
accuracy on graph-reasoning questions varies substantially with the choice
of text encoding when the graph and question are held fixed. We ask a
complementary question about the same benchmark: does prepending a short
\emph{primer} of derived, node-level facts to the graph encoding improve
the model's answers?

Four confounds complicate the interpretation of such an effect. First, a
primer may help because the model reads the answer from it rather than
reasoning over the graph (answer leakage). Second, a primer changes prompt
length, and context length and position alone affect retrieval and
reasoning accuracy \citep{liu2024lostinthemiddle,kamradt2023needle,
levy2024sametask}. Third, answer extraction can err in ways that correlate
with response style, which appears as a spurious condition effect
(Section~\ref{sec:extraction}). Fourth, a model near ceiling on a task
leaves no room for any primer effect to appear.

We address three research questions. \textbf{RQ1}: when a primer improves
accuracy, is the improvement attributable to the primer stating the
answer? \textbf{RQ2}: does added text affect accuracy independently of its
content? \textbf{RQ3}: does any primer that does not state the answer
improve accuracy beyond a length-matched control, and through what
mechanism? Our main contribution is the four controls of
Figure~\ref{fig:design} applied jointly --- a graph-blind shortcut solver
refit at the evaluated graph size (Section~\ref{sec:shortcuts}), a
length-matched \texttt{filler}, a minimal-length \texttt{components}, and a
degree-preserving rewiring (Section~\ref{sec:rewiring}) --- on $40$-node
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs across four model arms and six tasks.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

We adopt the GraphQA task definitions of \citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph}
and manipulate graph size and edge density directly, so that difficulty is
an experimental variable rather than an incidental one.
\citet{wang2023nlgraph} report that LLMs are brittle to spurious
correlations in graph problems and propose \emph{Build-a-Graph} and
\emph{Algorithmic} prompting, the prompt-level interventions closest to a
primer; \citet{perozzi2024letyourgraph} instead learn a continuous graph
encoding. \citet{dziri2023faithandfate} argue that transformers solve
compositional tasks by linearised subgraph matching rather than systematic
reasoning, which raises the same question as a primer's benefit: does it
reflect reasoning or a shorter pattern to match?
\citet{geirhos2020shortcut} frame this as \emph{shortcut learning}; our
shortcut solver operationalises that concern for the primer channel.
Irrelevant context degrades reasoning \citep{shi2023distracted}, as does
input length well below the context limit \citep{levy2024sametask}; our
\texttt{filler} condition measures this cost directly. The \texttt{rwse}
condition adapts the random-walk structural encoding of
\citet{dwivedi2022graph}, rendered as sentences, and the rewiring follows
the double-edge-swap configuration model \citep{fosdick2018configuring}. We
distinguish Qwen3's native thinking mode, toggled through the chat
template, from chain-of-thought \emph{prompting}
\citep{wei2022chainofthought}; this paper tests only the former.

A primer is a preamble of factual sentences about a graph's structure,
prepended to the standard text encoding and the task question
(Figure~\ref{fig:design}; a complete example appears in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-prompt}). A single renderer produces all seven
conditions, so they share sentence framing and number formatting and differ
only in the facts stated. The exception is \texttt{filler}, which states no
fact and therefore uses its own frame (``Node~$N$ is simply present within
the graph G.''); it is a length control, not a content control.
\texttt{rwse} states each node's random-walk return probabilities after two
and three steps \citep{dwivedi2022graph}.

\subsection{Shortcut solvers}
\label{sec:shortcuts}

A primer's measured benefit is evidence of graph reasoning only if it
exceeds what a solver that never sees the graph can recover from the
primer text. Our shortcut solver combines sixteen exact rules, one
heuristic, eight fitted rules (fit and scored on disjoint graph sets) and an
exact enumeration bound for small graphs. Its accuracy on a (task,
condition) cell, the \emph{shortcut bar}, is a content-only lower bound: it
never overstates what the primer text alone permits.

Bars are fit at the evaluated graph size: $n{=}40$ Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi
graphs at the seven densities of the sweep, with disjoint $300$-graph fit
and test sets per density (Table~\ref{tab:shortcut}); the held-out arms,
which answer the published $5$--$19$-node split, use bars fit on that split,
which differ substantially (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:shortcut-published}). A (task, condition) pair offers a
\emph{substitute route} when its bar exceeds the \texttt{none} bar by more
than $0.05$; every gain is either at most $0.011$ or at least $0.066$, so
any threshold between these values yields the same split. Only
\texttt{node\_degree} (under \texttt{rwse}, \texttt{degree}, \texttt{all})
and \texttt{edge\_count} (under \texttt{clustering}, \texttt{degree},
\texttt{all}) offer a route. \texttt{node\_count} and \texttt{cycle\_check}
have constant gold answers at $n{=}40$ ($40$, and ``yes'' at every density)
and are excluded.

\subsection{Rewiring: isolating structure from length}
\label{sec:rewiring}

\texttt{filler} controls length only approximately, and comparisons across
graph generators confound structure with class balance, so we use a
within-instance design. A double-edge swap replaces edges $(a,b),(c,d)$
with $(a,d),(c,b)$ \citep{fosdick2018configuring}, which preserves the
degree sequence, $n$, $m$, the \texttt{node\_degree} gold answer and the
prompt length while changing the triangle count that the
\texttt{clustering} primer reports. We generate \texttt{low},
\texttt{base} and \texttt{high} variants of each graph ($n{=}40$, mean
degree $12$, $200$ graphs per level; mean clustering $0.01$, $0.31$,
$0.73$) and evaluate \texttt{node\_degree} under \texttt{none},
\texttt{clustering} and \texttt{filler} with \texttt{qwen3-1.7b},
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think} and \texttt{qwen35-2b}. If the
\texttt{clustering} benefit derives from the stated values, it should vary
with triangle count. If instead the model reads mean clustering as a
density prior (in an Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graph $\bar{C}\approx p$), its
degree estimates should shift toward $\bar{C}\,(n-1)$: $0.5$, $12.0$ and
$28.7$ across levels, against a true mean of $12$.

\subsection{Answer extraction and measurement validity}
\label{sec:extraction}

Extraction errors can masquerade as condition effects, so answers are
extracted with task-specific patterns rather than a positional rule: on
\texttt{cycle\_check} a last-yes/no rule is not sufficient, because
\texttt{qwen3-4b} often answers correctly and then recites that a cycle has
``no repeated'' nodes, which a positional rule scores against one condition
more than another. A manual check of $50$ rows per arm confirmed that
\texttt{<think>} blocks never reach the extractor. Truncated pairs are
dropped; scoring them as incorrect changes the verdict on only three
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b} \texttt{cycle\_check} cells
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}).

\subsection{Significance testing}
\label{sec:significance}

Every comparison is paired on the same graph and question
\citep{dror2018hitchhikers} and tested with the exact McNemar test
\citep{mcnemar1947note}, Benjamini--Hochberg corrected
\citep{benjaminihochberg1995controlling} within each (arm, task)
family at $q{=}0.05$; a claim that a condition beats both controls also
requires significance under a global correction. For the effect--baseline
relation (Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}), baseline and effect are
estimated from disjoint random halves of each cell's graphs
\citep{chernozhukov2018double}, and the route $\times$ baseline
interaction is bootstrapped over (arm, task, density) blocks, which share
one \texttt{none} sample. For null cells we simulate a
minimum detectable effect (MDE) at power $0.8$, $\alpha{=}0.05$.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Models and tasks.} The six GraphQA tasks
\citep{fatemi2023talklikeagraph} are those named in
Table~\ref{tab:main}. An \emph{arm} is a model checkpoint with a fixed
decoding configuration; the main arms are Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B
\citep{qwen2025qwen3}, each with native thinking mode off
(\texttt{qwen3-1.7b}, \texttt{qwen3-4b}) and on (\texttt{-think}), which
gives both modes of the same weights at two sizes.

\paragraph{Sweep design.} All four arms answer one shared prompt set:
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs \citep{erdosrenyi1959random} with $n{=}40$ at
densities $p\in\{.10,.20,.35,.50\}$, $100$ graphs per (density, task) cell,
seven conditions and six tasks, for $16{,}800$ prompts per arm. Decoding is
greedy with a budget of $2{,}048$ new tokens for plain arms and $8{,}192$
for thinking arms; Section~\ref{sec:results-thinking} reports a
budget-matched comparison. A high-density extension covers
\texttt{node\_degree} and \texttt{edge\_existence} at $p\in\{.65,.75,.85\}$
($100$ graphs per cell, all four arms). For the one content effect that
survives the controls (Section~\ref{sec:results-clustering}), a dedicated
\texttt{node\_degree} sweep with \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} uses $400$ graphs per
density at all seven densities, and a replication uses $400$ newly seeded
graphs per density at $p\le.50$.

\paragraph{Held-out arms.} Eleven further arms, held out but not
pre-registered, were used for no claim reported here.\footnote{Eight
(Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, Gemma-4-E4B-it and Gemma-4-12B-it \citep{gemma4},
each with and without thinking) answer the published GraphQA split ($30$
graphs per cell, five tasks, six conditions); three (Qwen3-0.6B with and
without thinking, and Qwen3.5-2B \citep{qwen35}, \texttt{qwen35-2b})
answer a $100$-graph probe of \texttt{none} and \texttt{degree} on all six
tasks.}

\paragraph{Metrics.} Exact match for every task; mean absolute error for
the integer tasks is in the Appendix.\footnote{Set-F1 on
\texttt{connected\_nodes} is $0.96$--$1.00$ in every cell and does not
discriminate between conditions.}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Main sweep}
\label{sec:results-main}

\input{main_table.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the main sweep, and its largest entries are not
primer effects. They fall on \texttt{node\_count}, whose gold answer is the
same for every graph, and on the two truncated \texttt{edge\_count} cells,
both flagged in the table. Across the four remaining tasks the entries are
small and often change sign between the two model sizes. Two systematic
patterns account for most of them: a cost of added text, which
\texttt{filler} isolates (Section~\ref{sec:results-length}), and a benefit
that appears only where the primer states the answer
(Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}). Both thinking arms answer nearly
every question correctly under \texttt{none}, so the cells that can show an
effect at all belong to the plain arms.

Throughout, $\Delta$ is a paired difference against \texttt{none} in
percentage points, computed over instances in which neither condition was
truncated; per-cell bootstrap intervals are in the Appendix. Outside the
two flagged cells, every task truncates on fewer than $7\%$ of instances.

\subsection{Length is an active treatment}
\label{sec:results-length}

\texttt{filler} significantly reduces accuracy on
\texttt{connected\_nodes} and \texttt{edge\_existence} in both plain arms,
even though it states no fact about the graph. The cost grows with the
difficulty of the task. Over the full seven-density range on
\texttt{node\_degree} it is harmless up to $p{=}.35$ and then costs between
$9$ and $14$ points at the four densest levels, tracking accuracy under
\texttt{none} across levels almost perfectly ($r{=}{+}0.95$; $6.3$ points
pooled, $p{<}10^{-4}$). The cost of added text therefore grows in the same
direction as the benefit of an answer-stating primer
(Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}).

The \texttt{edge\_existence} effects are not changes in how the graph is
read. The hit rate stays at $99$--$100\%$ under every condition, so
accuracy moves only through the false-alarm rate, which \texttt{filler}
raises and \texttt{clustering} lowers. With hits at ceiling, sensitivity
and criterion cannot be separated
\citep{macmillan2005detection,hautus1995corrections} (Appendix
Tables~\ref{tab:booleanbias} and~\ref{tab:sdt}). The \texttt{node\_count}
gains have a simpler explanation. The dominant error under \texttt{none} is
an off-by-one, induced by the encoder's ``nodes $0,\dots,39$'' preamble and
given by $68.5\%$ of \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} answers, and almost any interposed
text reduces it.

\subsection{Answer-stating primers: route substitution}
\label{sec:results-baseline}

\label{fig:effects}
\end{figure}

A \emph{cell} is an (arm, task, density, condition) combination with at
least ten usable pairs. On the main sweep plus the high-density extension,
route cells gain where accuracy under \texttt{none} is low and lose where
it is high, while no-route cells show no such relation
(Figure~\ref{fig:crossfit}; binned means also in Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:baseline}). Baseline and effect share sampling noise, and a
difference in significance is not a significant difference
\citep{gelman2006difference}, so we estimate the route $\times$ baseline
interaction on cross-fitted halves of each cell's graphs: $-11.0$ points
per unit of baseline, with logit-scale and windowed robustness in Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:robust}. The relation is not graded. Restricted to cells
whose baseline lies between $0.10$ and $0.90$ it disappears altogether, so
what the data support is a contrast between floor and ceiling rather than a
smooth dependence on competence: a stated answer helps a model that cannot
compute the answer and hurts one that already can. Our coverage is thinnest
exactly where a graded relation would have to show itself. Manipulating
difficulty directly reproduces the contrast. For
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think} on \texttt{node\_degree}, raising density from
$p{=}.10$ to $.85$ drives accuracy under \texttt{none} down and
\texttt{degree}'s benefit up almost monotonically, from $+3.1$ to $+34.2$
points (Figure~\ref{fig:effects}a).

\paragraph{Held-out arms and renaming.}
\label{sec:results-heldout}
Three checks on the eleven held-out arms point the same way. The
interaction has the same sign and direction there, though it is estimated
far less precisely (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:robust}). Substituting the
other arms' accuracy on a cell for the cell's own baseline removes the
relation, so it tracks a model's own competence rather than the difficulty
of the item. Replacing integer node identifiers with Game-of-Thrones names
leaves \texttt{degree}'s effect essentially unchanged, so it does not
depend on integer labels. None of this separates copying a stated value
from computing the answer, but one sign of copying is that when a model
errs despite a stated answer, the value it gives is its list-neighbour's
stated degree far more often than chance allows ($p{=}.002$).

\subsection{Non-answer content: clustering on node\_degree}
\label{sec:results-clustering}

\texttt{clustering} on \texttt{node\_degree} is the only primer that does
not state the answer and still exceeds both controls. This cell and the
$p{=}.35$--$.50$ window below were chosen after the main sweep, so the cell
was re-run with $400$ graphs per density and an independent-seed
replication that played no part in either choice
(Figure~\ref{fig:effects}b; Appendix
Tables~\ref{tab:headline} and~\ref{tab:headline-density}). Pooled over all
seven densities the gain over \texttt{none} is small ($+1.9$ points), and
the apparently larger gain over \texttt{filler} mostly reflects
\texttt{filler}'s own cost, which accounts for the whole difference at the
densest levels. The effect is concentrated in the middle of the density
range. Pooled over $p{=}.35$ and $.50$, \texttt{clustering} exceeds
\texttt{none} by $+5.9$ points ($95\%$ CI $[2.1, 9.8]$) and \texttt{filler}
by a similar margin, while \texttt{filler} and \texttt{none} do not differ
there, so length does not account for it. The replication gives $+5.5$ over
the same two densities, and per-density estimates are too imprecise to
localise the effect more finely. \texttt{qwen3-4b} is at ceiling in the
main sweep but shows the effect on the high-density extension, under the
global correction against both controls; the thinking arms do not show it
at all.

Neither mechanism we can test explains the effect. Under rewiring, mean
clustering rises from $0.01$ to $0.73$ while the benefit stays flat, and
for \texttt{qwen35-2b} \texttt{filler} matches or exceeds
\texttt{clustering} at every level (Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:rewiring}). The density-prior account fails as well.
Signed error under \texttt{clustering} should shift by up to $17$ at the
\texttt{high} level, but the paired shift stays within $0.3$ of zero at
every level for all three arms (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:prior}).

\subsection{Thinking mode buys accuracy with budget}
\label{sec:results-thinking}
\label{sec:results-power}

Thinking brings most cells to ceiling, and where it does not, truncation
censors the task. Part of its advantage is the larger output budget rather
than the reasoning it buys. Rescored at the plain arms' $2{,}048$-token
budget, which is exact under greedy decoding, the 1.7B thinking arm still
wins on \texttt{edge\_existence} ($97.8$ against $70.5\%$) but loses on
\texttt{cycle\_check} ($38.0$ against $90.7\%$; Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:budget}).

Many of the main-sweep nulls are uninformative. Accuracy under
\texttt{none} is already at floor or ceiling in a large share of the null
cells of every arm, from $42\%$ in \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} to all $33$ in
\texttt{qwen3-4b-think}, so no effect in at least one direction could be
detected there at any sample size. Where a gain could be detected, the
median detectable size is $5.6$ points for the 1.7B arms and $10.0$ for
4B.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Structural primers change LLM accuracy on GraphQA in two ways. For RQ1, a
primer that states the requested quantity acts as a second route to the
answer, helping a model at floor and harming one at ceiling; the contrast
survives cross-fitting, a logit transformation, held-out arms, a
negative-control difficulty estimate and node renaming, but is not graded
at intermediate baselines. For RQ2, added text is an active treatment whose
cost exceeds most content effects and grows with difficulty, so a primer
compared only against \texttt{none} confounds content with length. For
RQ3, the one surviving effect of non-answer content is small, concentrated
at intermediate densities and unexplained. All claims are behavioural.
Evaluations of prompt-level graph augmentations should report baseline
accuracy, a length-matched control and a graph-blind solver.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The main sweep covers two model sizes from one family; the held-out arms
extend the range but on a smaller corpus with fewer conditions and were not
pre-registered. The route classification depends on a threshold and on the
$n{=}40$ shortcut refit, and the route relation is not detectable within
the $0.10$--$0.90$ baseline window, where data are sparse. All graphs are
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi, in which local clustering is nearly uniform, so the
\texttt{clustering} primer carries little node-specific information; the
rewiring varies triangle count within this family but does not test other
families. The length control matches \texttt{clustering} to within $8\%$ in
tokens but is much shorter than \texttt{rwse} and \texttt{all}. Every prompt
uses one encoding (\texttt{incident}), one template and one primer
position, with greedy decoding. Several results rest on $400$ or fewer
instances per cell; an MDE is reported for every null result, but it does
not replace a larger sample. Three further primers would address the main
open questions: \emph{shuffled-value} (a stricter placebo than
\texttt{filler}), \emph{corrupted} (to separate copying from computation)
and \emph{single-fact} (to separate interference from route substitution).

\section*{Ethics Statement}

This work evaluates open-weight language models on synthetically generated
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs. It involves no human subjects, no personal or
identifying data and no annotation labour. We see no direct risk of harm
from the findings; the principal cost is the GPU compute for approximately
$200{,}000$ generations.
