\begin{abstract}
Language models are increasingly used to reason over structured data, yet a graph written out as text is still hard for them to read, and their accuracy depends on how it is written. Graph neural networks benefit from explicit structural features, but it is unclear whether writing the same statistics into a textual graph helps a frozen language model execute graph queries. We prepend node degree, local clustering coefficients, and random-walk return probabilities to GraphQA's incident encoding and evaluate Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B, with and without thinking, across $84{,}000$ responses on $700$ paired graphs. Stated degrees improve node-degree accuracy for both Qwen3-1.7B modes but reduce it for plain Qwen3-4B, which already counts accurately. This benefit does not establish improved graph reading: a graph-blind solver that never reads the edge list answers degree and edge-count queries at $100\%$ accuracy, against its own $14.4\%$ and $3.0\%$ without a primer. Nor are stated answers always used: with the degree printed in its prompt, plain Qwen3-1.7B is correct on $43\%$ of degree prompts, against $39\%$ without it. Clustering and return probabilities, which do not directly state the answer, yield inconsistent effects, with no significant gain in joint degree and neighbor-set accuracy. A near-constant component-count sentence also shifts how Qwen3-4B answers, raising its exact neighbor-set accuracy. Correct answers need not rest on the graph: $43.5\%$ of plain Qwen3-1.7B's correct cycle answers without a primer cite a nonexistent edge. Explicit statistics can help by supplying answers or changing how a model uses the prompt, but do not consistently improve graph-query execution.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/GalBrk/GraphTalk}} % [main] [joint] [comp] [compproc] [ccanswer] [recover] [setup] [bars] [blindbar]
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Structural features and positional encodings improve graph neural networks and graph Transformers by supplying information that message passing may not readily recover \citep{dwivedi2022graph,rampasek2022recipe}. A language model given a graph as text must instead extract structure from node and edge descriptions. In \emph{Talk like a Graph}, \citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph} report that LLMs find basic graph tasks difficult and that performance depends on the encoding, task, and graph structure; NLGraph likewise finds that prompting becomes less effective on more complex problems \citep{wang2023nlgraph}. We ask: \emph{when a black-box language model reads a textual graph, do explicit structural statistics improve execution of graph queries?}

Prior work varies how graphs are serialized or supplies learned graph tokens and structural prompts (Section~\ref{sec:related}). These studies leave a narrower question open: whether precomputed statistics help a model answer graph queries when the textual edge list and question are fixed. Answering it requires care: a degree sentence can itself contain the answer to a degree query, so an accuracy gain need not indicate better use of the edges.

We test this question by placing a primer of degree, local clustering coefficients, or random-walk return probabilities before GraphQA's incident encoding (Figure~\ref{fig:prompt}). We evaluate Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B, with and without thinking, on paired graph--question prompts across six GraphQA tasks. We also test the combined statistics, a component-count sentence, and a structure-free filler. A graph-blind solver, which never reads the edge list, gives a lower bound on what the primer makes available; paired comparisons, joint degree and neighbor-set correctness, and checks of response claims against the graph provide further evidence about how answers are obtained.

Our contribution is a controlled evaluation of these statistics as textual primers for graph queries, with controls for answer supply and the effects of added text. This framework examines whether a change in accuracy reflects information given directly by a primer or improved use of the graph's incident encoding. We find that stated degrees help mainly by supplying the answer, while statistics that do not supply it change answers inconsistently.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Graph representations for LLMs.} \emph{Talk like a Graph} introduces GraphQA and studies how textual encodings, prompts, and graph structure affect graph-query accuracy \citep{fatemi2023talklikeagraph}; NLGraph evaluates natural-language graph problems and prompting methods \citep{wang2023nlgraph}. Other approaches supply learned graph tokens \citep{perozzi2024letyourgraph}, motif descriptions \citep{das2024modality}, or structural context for node classification on text-attributed graphs \citep{huang2024can}. Our experiment holds the incident encoding and question fixed while varying named statistics in plain text, including whether a statistic supplies the queried answer.

\paragraph{Controls and response evidence.} Added context, input length, and formatting can change model answers \citep{shi2023distracted,levy2024sametask,sclar2024quantifying}. A successful partial-input baseline exposes a shortcut, but an unsuccessful one cannot exclude other shortcuts \citep{feng2019misleading}. Generated explanations may also misrepresent how an answer was reached \citep{turpin2023unfaithful}. We therefore compare primers with a structure-free filler, measure what a graph-blind solver can recover, and check stated graph claims against the actual edges.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\paragraph{Models and graphs.} We evaluate Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B \citep{qwen2025qwen3}, each with thinking off (P) and on (T); each model--mode pair is an \emph{arm}. We use zero-shot greedy decoding and one response per prompt (budgets in Appendix~\ref{app:audit}). A pilot on published GraphQA graphs left larger models near ceiling (Appendix~\ref{app:pilot}). We therefore use $40$-node independent-edge graphs $G(n,p)$ \citep{gilbert1959random} at $p\in\{.10,.20,.35,.50\}$, extending node-degree and edge-existence tests to $p\in\{.65,.75,.85\}$. Every condition and arm receives the same $100$ graphs per task and density, allowing paired comparisons. % [setup]

\paragraph{Tasks and primers.} We retain GraphQA's incident encoding and six tasks (Figure~\ref{fig:prompt}). Answers to node degree, connected nodes, edge count, and edge existence vary across graphs. The other two tasks have constant answers: every graph has $40$ nodes and contains a cycle, so we use cycle-check responses to assess the support given for a ``yes'' answer. We score connected nodes primarily by exact match of the predicted and true neighbor sets; set-F1 is a secondary measure that gives partial credit. % [setup]

Before the encoding, we place either no primer; one sentence per node stating its degree, local clustering coefficient, or two- and three-step random-walk return probabilities (a truncated random-walk structural encoding, RWSE; \citealp{dwivedi2022graph,rampasek2022recipe}); or a sentence combining all three statistics. We add two controls: a sentence giving the number of connected components, and a filler that names each node without stating a statistic. The filler is similar in length to clustering but not to the other primers (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:primerlength}). A rule-based graph-blind solver tests what can be answered from the primer without the edge list; it also receives the node and edge counts but never returns them as answers.

\paragraph{Outcomes and inference.} Each response is classified as correct, wrong, or truncated; accuracy uses all prompts as its denominator. The primary effect is the paired change in accuracy, in percentage points, against no primer and against filler on the $400$ main-sweep graphs per task (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}). We use exact McNemar tests \citep{mcnemar1947note}, bootstrap intervals over graphs, and Benjamini--Hochberg correction \citep{benjaminihochberg1995controlling} within each arm--task--control family. Cells with at least $15\%$ truncation in either condition are flagged and excluded from pooled summaries. We also report balanced accuracy for edge existence because its positive-answer rate varies with density. Plain--thinking comparisons use the same graphs; we call the modes equivalent only when the $90\%$ interval for their difference lies within $\pm3$ points. A non-significant effect is not evidence of no effect (detectable sizes in Appendix~\ref{app:audit}).

\paragraph{Evidence of graph use.} Because degree and neighbor-set questions target the same node, their joint correctness tests whether a degree gain accompanies correct identification of that node's neighbors; it does not establish the model's internal procedure. Rule-based classifiers identify direct-answer and neighbor-list response patterns, primer conflicts, and graph claims. For correct, finished cycle-check answers, we distinguish a cited cycle whose steps are all edges, a claimed cycle containing a nonexistent edge, a walk that is not a cycle, and an answer without an explicit cycle. Within the last category, we also identify valid arguments from edge count: a $40$-node graph with at least $40$ edges must contain a cycle. Conflict detection was compared with blind hand labels; the other classifiers were spot-checked by an LLM under human supervision (Appendix~\ref{app:audit}).

\paragraph{Follow-ups.} We repeat Qwen3-1.7B node-degree tests on $400$ graphs per density across all seven densities and run fresh-seed and $20$--$160$-node checks at mean degrees $8$ and $16$; the size grid also includes Qwen3-8B. % [ddplain] [ddrep] [fixdeg17]

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{What the primers make available}
\label{sec:states}
From stated degrees alone, the graph-blind solver recovers node degree directly and edge count by halving the degree sum, both with $100\%$ accuracy. Without degrees, the rule-based solver reaches at most $21.0\%$ on degree and $10.4\%$ on edge count, compared with $14.4\%$ and $3.0\%$ without a primer (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:solver}). % [bars] [blindbar]
On sparse graphs, however, a lookup fitted on separate graphs recovers degree from rounded clustering or RWSE values substantially better than the majority answer (Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). In the models' responses, the largest main-sweep gains on the four variable-answer tasks occur when degrees supply the answer or its terms; primers without degrees and the controls produce effects of either sign (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}; Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:effects}). % [main]

\subsection{Stated degrees can help or disrupt counting}
\label{sec:readoff}
The degree primer raises node-degree accuracy by $7.5$ points for plain 1.7B and $8.8$ for thinking 1.7B, but lowers it by $6.5$ for plain 4B, whose unaided accuracy is $99.25\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}). % [main]
Its effect depends on the cost of counting from the incident list. Within plain 4B, it harms queries about the lowest-degree nodes and helps those about the highest-degree nodes ($-11.8$ and $+10.6$ points across seven densities); % [pdnode]
at $p=.85$, where unaided accuracy falls to $33.0\%$, it gains $27.0$ points (Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). % [flip]
Stated degrees also supply every term needed for edge count, raising plain 4B's main-sweep accuracy from $2.00\%$ to $29.25\%$, far below the solver's $100\%$. % [main]

Stated answers are not always used. Across seven densities, plain 1.7B is correct on $43\%$ of degree prompts under the degree primer, against $39\%$ without it; none of the answers the primer turns from wrong to correct at $p=.20$ and $.35$ cites the stated value. Plain 4B, by contrast, appears to look the answer up: at $p=.50$, $62\%$ of its responses answer directly and only $8\%$ list the neighbors; across densities, its wrong lookups match the degree printed one sentence away more often than chance (Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). % [recover] [plaincite] [route] [copyerr]
Its losses under the degree primer are concentrated on prompts where the response pattern changes. % [routesplit]

\subsection{Primers also change answers they do not supply}
\label{sec:side}
Filler alone flips up to $18.6\%$ of an arm's outcomes against no primer, showing that added text can matter without adding a statistic (Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). % [churn]
Clustering and RWSE mostly have smaller main-sweep effects than degree, varying in sign by task and model. On plain 4B's edge-existence queries, clustering raises balanced accuracy by $3.0$ points while RWSE lowers it by $6.5$ (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ee}). % [eemain]
Clustering's $3.0$-point gain on plain 1.7B degree queries is not significant in Table~\ref{tab:matrix} but replicates on additional low-density graphs but is not detected at high density or in Qwen3-8B (Appendix~\ref{app:pilot}). % [main]
Combining the statistics can reverse their separate effects: on plain 4B's dense degree queries, degree, clustering, and RWSE each raise accuracy ($9.3$, $11.3$, and $7.7$ points), but the combined primer lowers it by $21.3$ on the same graphs (Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). % [main] [clusthi]

On the four variable-answer tasks, the largest unflagged main-sweep gain without degrees is the component sentence's $9.2$-point increase in plain 4B's \emph{exact} neighbor-set accuracy; its set-F1 barely moves (Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). The sentence is identical across all sampled graphs at $p\geq.20$, yet responses restate the queried node's incident line much more often with it than with no primer or filler ($98.5\%$, $63.0\%$, and $21.8\%$). Filler moves this response pattern in the opposite direction and lowers exact-match accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}). % [main] [comp] [compproc]

\subsection{Can a primer close the gap to thinking?}
\label{sec:vsthink}
Where thinking outperforms plain mode, primers rarely close the gap, and for plain 4B several widen it (Table~\ref{tab:vsthink}). Among the exceptions, the component sentence brings plain 4B's neighbor-set exact match within the $\pm3$-point equivalence margin of thinking 4B without a primer, while filler widens that gap; the combined primer reduces plain 1.7B's node-degree gap from $15.8$ to $5.5$ points; and under degree, plain 1.7B's neighbor-set gap is not significant, though its interval extends beyond the margin. % [vsthink]

The degree primer puts plain 4B ahead of thinking 4B on edge count, but the comparison mostly reflects completion: thinking 4B is correct on $97.6\%$ of the edge-count responses it finishes, while $79.50\%$ of its responses exhaust the token budget. % [edgecount]

\subsection{Primers in thinking mode}
\label{sec:thinkmode}
Thinking 4B is near ceiling on degree, neighbor sets, and edge existence; every primer changes these accuracies by at most $2.5$ points, which says little about harder cases. % [null4bt]
Thinking 1.7B gains from stated degrees but often lists neighbors and checks its count against the printed value. This checking raises its node-degree truncation rate from $1.1\%$ to $7.6\%$ across seven densities; when its count and the printed value conflict, it ends with the stated value in $66\%$ of finished cases. % [trunc] [pdsource]
Truncation also shapes thinking 4B's results: in every comparison, most changed answers involve a response that ran out of tokens (Appendix~\ref{app:detail}), so some apparent primer effects reflect whether a response finishes. % [churnwhy]

\subsection{Do correct answers have graph support?}
\label{sec:reston}
Clustering and RWSE do not significantly improve the joint correctness of degree and neighbor-set answers for the same node in any arm (Appendix~\ref{app:detail}). The degree primer raises this measure by $8.5$ points for plain 1.7B but lowers it by $9.2$ for plain 4B; the component sentence raises plain 4B's by $9.2$. % [joint]

Because cycle-check accuracy cannot measure cycle detection here (Section~\ref{sec:method}), we instead examine the support in correct, finished ``yes'' responses (Table~\ref{tab:cycles}). Without a primer, $43.5\%$ of plain 1.7B's correct, finished responses cite an invented cycle containing a nonexistent edge. % [ccanswer]
Counting over all prompts, valid cited cycles or edge-count arguments support $34.2\%$ and $58.5\%$ for plain 1.7B and plain 4B, respectively. % [ccvalid]
The degree primer shifts plain-model responses toward valid edge-count arguments, which need no named cycle; plain 1.7B's share of prompts with valid support rises to $50.2\%$, although its cycle-check accuracy falls by $17.5$ points as truncation rises by $8.2$. Thus, a correct ``yes'' can be accompanied by a valid proof, an invalid claimed cycle, or no explicit cycle; the label alone does not distinguish them. % [ccvalid] [main]

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Structural statistics serve a different role here than in graph models, where random-walk encodings can distinguish structures that some message-passing networks cannot represent \citep{xu2019powerful,dwivedi2022graph}. In our setting, the incident encoding already contains every edge, but the language model must find the relevant edges and reason over them. Stated degree can bypass that computation for degree and edge-count queries, but it also introduces a lookup that can fail when the model selects the wrong node's sentence. Whether it helps therefore depends partly on the model's unaided accuracy and its ability to use the stated value.

Clustering and RWSE, by contrast, do not directly answer the studied queries, and their mixed effects, together with changes caused by filler and the near-constant component sentence, are consistent with primers changing how models use the prompt. The independent-edge graphs provide limited opportunity for these statistics to convey task-relevant structure beyond degree and density; the results do not establish how they would behave on graph families where triangles or return patterns are more informative.

Finally, answer accuracy and the stated justification measure different things. Joint degree and neighbor-set correctness gives evidence about use of the incident line, while cycle checks reveal whether a correct ``yes'' is supported by a valid graph argument.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}
Corrupting or omitting queried-node degrees while holding graphs fixed would test reliance on the primer versus counting. Shuffled labels and primer order, and a length- and position-matched neutral substitute for the component sentence, would test presentation effects. Acyclic graphs and graph families varying in triangle counts and return patterns at matched size and density would test cycle detection and structural primers. Other model families and token budgets would assess generality.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We asked whether explicit structural statistics improve a black-box language model's execution of queries over a textual graph. In paired GraphQA tests, stated degrees produced the clearest gains, chiefly where the model counted unreliably but could use the stated value; they could also disrupt accurate counting. Clustering and random-walk returns produced task- and density-dependent effects without a consistent gain in joint degree and neighbor-set correctness. Controls and graph-checked responses showed why accuracy alone is insufficient: added text can change answers, and even a correct cycle label can cite a nonexistent edge. The value of a textual statistic depends on the query, what the statistic gives away, and what the model can already compute.

\section*{Limitations}
We test two Qwen3 models (Qwen3-8B in one follow-up), one incident encoding, fixed prompts, and mostly $40$-node independent-edge graphs; results may differ where triangles or return patterns are more informative. All sampled graphs contain cycles, so cycle analysis evaluates support for ``yes'' answers, not cycle discrimination. The filler only roughly matches clustering's length, and component count supplies structural information. The graph-blind solver cannot exclude other clues. We did not test incorrect or shuffled statistics or an instruction-only control; the queried node's list position and identifier length are confounded with each other. Fixed budgets cause substantial truncation in some thinking-mode cells, and reruns can differ. Response categories describe behavior, not internal computation: only conflict classifiers were checked against blind hand labels; the others received supervised spot checks.

\section{Method Details}
\label{app:audit}

\paragraph{Primer statistics.} For the undirected adjacency matrix $A$, let $d_v=\sum_u A_{vu}$ and $P_{vu}=A_{vu}/d_v$ when $d_v>0$ (a zero row otherwise). If $t_v$ is the number of edges among $v$'s neighbors, local clustering is $c_v=2t_v/[d_v(d_v-1)]$, set to zero for $d_v<2$. The two return features are $r_v^{(k)}=(P^k)_{vv}$ for $k\in\{2,3\}$. Floats are printed to two decimals, with one sentence per node in node order (Table~\ref{tab:primerlength}).

\paragraph{Feature choice.} Degree and random-walk encodings are used in graph Transformers \citep{rampasek2022recipe}; clustering records edges among a node's neighbors. We use return steps 2 and 3: step 1 is zero in a simple graph, while step 3 reflects triangles. We omit Laplacian coordinates because their signs and degenerate eigenspace bases require a convention \citep{dwivedi2022graph}. Component count is a global control, though it is informative in principle: a graph with $n$ nodes, $m$ edges and $c$ components is acyclic exactly when $m=n-c$.

\paragraph{Models, budgets and inference.} We run the Qwen3 checkpoints with \texttt{transformers}, greedy decoding and one response per prompt. The output limit is $8{,}192$ tokens, except $2{,}048$ for plain arms at $p\geq.65$; none of these shorter-budget cells truncates above $2.5\%$. The main sweep and extension comprise $84{,}000$ responses on $700$ graphs. % [setup] [extract]
In one $100$-graph cell the median smallest detectable paired effect is $7.4$ points, and $13.7$ for side statistics at $25$--$75\%$ baseline accuracy. % [power]
Under one BH correction over all $264$ tests, every bold cell of Table~\ref{tab:matrix} but one and all but three $^{\mathrm{f}}$ marks stay significant. % [globalbh]
Even greedy reruns change correctness on $5.3\%$ of identical prompts. Paired effects therefore compare net correct shares; the number of responses that switch outcome is a separate descriptive measure. % [rerun]

\paragraph{Graph-blind solver.} It receives the printed primer and the query targets, plus the graph's node and edge counts as auxiliary facts; it never reads the incident edge list. It does not return a granted count as the answer: edge count is derived from stated degrees. It uses $16$ exact rules, one heuristic and eight fitted rules; fitting and evaluation use disjoint graphs. Its score shows what one shortcut achieves, not an upper bound on what the full prompt conveys (Table~\ref{tab:solver}). % [bars] [leak]

\paragraph{Stated answers (\S\ref{sec:readoff}).} The effect varies with unaided accuracy (Figures~\ref{fig:degreeeffect} and~\ref{fig:headroom}). In node-degree cells answered $25$--$50\%$ and $50$--$75\%$ correctly without a primer, answer-stating primers gain $16.1$ and $12.9$ points; at $75$--$90\%$ they lose $5.8$. These bands hold on held-out halves of the graphs and with the truncation flag at $10\%$ or $50\%$ (at least $+12.5$ in every case). % [bands] [splithalf] [bandsens]
An effect mostly follows how well the model does unaided. At a fixed accuracy without a primer, density adds little ($p=.28$ for answer-stating primers, $p=.85$ for side statistics), except that side statistics' effect on node degree falls with density ($p=.006$). % [dxbase]
One model shows both effects. The degree primer costs plain 4B $12.0$ points at $p=.50$, where it answers $99.0\%$ correctly unaided, and gains $27.0$ at $p=.85$, where it answers $33.0\%$ (both $q<.01$). % [flip]

\paragraph{Graph support (\S\ref{sec:reston}).} Neither clustering nor RWSE significantly changes joint degree--neighbor correctness in any arm ($q\geq.16$). Plain 4B's is $85.75\%$ with no primer, $76.50\%$ with degree and $95.00\%$ with the component sentence; the degree primer raises plain 1.7B's from $54.00\%$ to $62.50\%$. % [joint]

With thinking and no primer, valid arguments back $78.0\%$ and $95.8\%$ of all prompts. The degree primer raises plain 4B's valid share from $58.5\%$ to $76.5\%$. Truncation accounts for $21.2$ of the $21.8$ points it costs thinking 1.7B but only $8.2$ of the $17.5$ it costs plain 1.7B (Tables~\ref{tab:full} and~\ref{tab:truncmatrix}). RWSE, all three statistics and filler lower plain 4B's valid share to $33.2\%$, $45.2\%$ and $45.8\%$ ($q<.05$). % [ccvalid] [main] [trunc]

\paragraph{Node count.} Because the encoding lists all $40$ ids, this constant-answer task cannot establish graph reading. Plain 1.7B answers $39$, the last id, on $68.5\%$ of finished responses without a primer and on $1.2\%$ under clustering, hence Table~\ref{tab:full}'s large node-count effects. % [nodecount] [ncanswer]

\section{Pilot and the Clustering Screen}
\label{app:pilot}
\paragraph{Pilot.} On the published GraphQA graphs ($5$--$19$ nodes), Gemma~4 E4B/12B \citep{gemma4} and Qwen3-8B/14B, in both modes, were near ceiling without a primer on most tasks. Edge count remained difficult. These pilot results motivated the 40-node graphs and smaller models; they are not pooled with the main sweep.

\paragraph{The clustering effect.} Clustering on plain 1.7B's node degree emerged from a $40$-cell screen, so Table~\ref{tab:clust} tests it on graphs the screen did not see, on a fresh seed, against filler, on $20$--$160$-node graphs at fixed mean degree, at high density and on Qwen3-8B. The low-density gain appears on unscreened graphs, a fresh seed and the fixed-mean-degree grid, but is not detected at high density or in Qwen3-8B (Table~\ref{tab:clust}). The screened-graph row is descriptive, because it reuses graphs from the initial search. No sampled response explicitly invokes clustering, and the gain does not vary detectably with the queried node's coefficient ($p=.50$). These observations motivate a position-based explanation but do not prove one. % [rqbehaviour] [rqhetero] [rqselection] [ddrep] [fixdeg8]
The clustering contrast is $+5.01$, $+0.75$ and $-0.11$ points for queried nodes in the first, middle and last third of the list, pooled across seven densities. A regression adjusting for degree, clustering and density estimates $1.74$ points less gain per ten later positions ($p=.018$); filler and component controls show no corresponding gradient. The clustering format resolves $224$ prior neighbor-list copying errors and introduces $176$ new ones. These associations do not isolate the cause of the gain; shuffling node order would test it directly. % [rqhetero] [rqbehaviour]
Components and filler also cut the neighbors the model misses ($0.94$ and $1.05$ per response at $p=.35$--$.50$, against $0.86$ with clustering and $1.49$ with none). % [rqbehaviour] [rqhetero]
