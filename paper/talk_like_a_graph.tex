\begin{abstract}
Prepending a \emph{primer} of factual sentences about a graph's structure
--- node degree, clustering coefficient, a random-walk structural encoding,
or component membership --- before its text encoding is a natural way to
ease graph-reasoning prompts for a language model. Any apparent gain is
illusory if a solver can recover the answer from the primer text without
consulting the graph, and confounded if the primer merely lengthens the
prompt. We evaluate four controls together: a graph-blind shortcut solver
refit at the evaluation graph size, a length-matched content-free filler, a
length-free content control, and a degree-preserving rewiring that varies
triangle count while holding the prompt string length constant. On four
arms (\texttt{qwen3-1.7b}, \texttt{qwen3-4b}, each with and without native
thinking mode) at $n{=}40$, primer effects are organised chiefly by the
model's own no-primer accuracy: where the primer offers a substitute route
to the answer, effect size falls with baseline ($r{=}{-0.31}$, $232$
cross-fitted cells, $p{=}1.8{\times}10^{-6}$); where it does not, there is
no relation ($r{=}{+0.02}$, $762$ cells). This reproduces on eleven
held-out arms spanning $0.6$--$14$B and a second model family
($r{=}{-0.674}$ over $105$ route cells vs. $r{=}{-0.186}$ over $93$
no-route cells) and, separately, under Game-of-Thrones node renaming
($r{=}0.933$ between the two naming schemes' effect sizes for the
route-offering primer). One non-answer-leaking effect ---
\texttt{clustering} on \texttt{node\_degree} --- beats the length-matched
filler across five of seven density levels ($+4.3$pp pooled, $p{=}2.4
{\times}10^{-8}$) and, at high density where the larger model is off
ceiling, replicates in \texttt{qwen3-4b} too ($+11.3$pp vs. \texttt{none},
$+9.3$pp vs. filler). A rewiring test that holds prompt length and degree
sequence fixed while permuting triangle count does not show the benefit
tracking the clustering values themselves. Two validity threats turn out to
explain much of the rest of the sweep: \texttt{edge\_existence} accuracy
moves mainly through a yes/no response bias (hit rate $99$--$100\%$
throughout; false-alarm rate swinging $7.5$--$61.8\%$ by condition), and
\texttt{node\_count}'s largest apparent gains fix an off-by-one artifact on
a task whose gold answer is constant at this graph size. We report every
result against a Benjamini--Hochberg correction and, for null cells, a
minimum detectable effect, so an absence of significance is distinguished
from a demonstrated absence of effect.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph} show that a large language model's
accuracy on graph-reasoning questions depends substantially on how the
graph is encoded as text, independent of reasoning ability, by varying the
encoding function while holding the question and graph fixed. This work
asks a different question about the same benchmark: does prepending a short
\emph{primer} of derived, node-level facts before the graph encoding help a
model answer questions about that graph?

Two confounds make such an effect hard to read. If a primer helps because
the model reads the answer out of the preamble rather than reasoning over
the graph, that is answer leakage, not reasoning. If it helps or hurts
because it changes prompt length, that says nothing about content:
\citet{liu2024lostinthemiddle} and the needle-in-a-haystack protocol of
\citet{kamradt2023needle} show that context length and position alone move
retrieval accuracy. A third, quieter confound is measurement: an extraction
bug that correlates with response style manifests as a condition effect
with nothing in the resulting table that looks wrong (Section
\ref{sec:extraction}). A fourth is headroom: once a model is near ceiling
on a task, no primer effect --- helpful or harmful --- has room to show up.

We build a control battery to separate these from a genuine structural
effect: a graph-blind shortcut solver refit at the graph size actually
evaluated (Section~\ref{sec:shortcuts}), a length-matched content-free
\texttt{filler} condition, a length-free content control
(\texttt{components}), and a degree-preserving rewiring that moves triangle
count while holding the rendered prompt byte-identical in length
(Section~\ref{sec:rewiring}). Our contributions:
(i) this control battery, applied together rather than one at a time;
(ii) an empirical map of where primers move accuracy on $n{=}40$
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs, four model arms, six tasks;
(iii) a demonstration that answer-stating primers act as a second route to
the answer whose value is set by the model's own baseline accuracy, with
cross-fitted, cluster-bootstrapped evidence and two independent
replications (held-out arms, node renaming); and
(iv) one non-answer-leaking content effect, replicated within one model,
across model scale in a baseline-matched regime, and constrained --- not
confirmed --- by a rewiring test of its mechanism.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

We adopt the GraphQA task definitions of \citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph}
and fix graph size and edge density experimentally (Section
\ref{sec:setup}) rather than sampling structure incidentally, so that
difficulty is a manipulated variable. \citet{wang2023nlgraph} report LLMs
are brittle to spurious correlations in graph and problem structure;
\citet{guo2023gpt4graph} find models struggle to exploit graph description
languages directly; \citet{jin2024largelanguagemodelsongraphs} survey the
wider LLM-graph literature. \citet{dziri2023faithandfate} argue
transformers solve compositional tasks by linearised subgraph matching
rather than systematic reasoning, which is the same question a primer's
apparent benefit raises: is a gain evidence of reasoning, or of a shorter
pattern to match? \citet{geirhos2020shortcut} frame this as \emph{shortcut
learning}; our shortcut solver (Section~\ref{sec:shortcuts}) operationalises
that concern for the primer channel specifically. The \texttt{rwse}
condition adapts the random-walk structural encoding of
\citet{dwivedi2022graph}, rendered as sentences rather than fed to an
attention mechanism. Our degree-preserving rewiring
(Section~\ref{sec:rewiring}) follows the double-edge-swap configuration
model of \citet{fosdick2018configuring}. Paired significance testing follows
\citet{dror2018hitchhikers}. We distinguish the ``thinking'' arms evaluated
here --- Qwen3's native thinking mode, toggled via the chat template ---
from chain-of-thought \emph{prompting} \citep{wei2022chainofthought}; the
two are not the same intervention, and we do not claim this paper tests the
latter.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Primers}
\label{sec:primers}

A primer is a preamble of factual sentences about a graph's local or global
structure, prepended to the standard text encoding and the task question. A
single renderer produces all seven conditions, so every condition shares
identical sentence framing and formatting for the facts it states (the
degenerate case, \textbf{filler}, states no fact and therefore uses its own
frame, ``Node~$N$ is simply present\dots''; this is a length control, not a
content one, by design). \textbf{none} is the graph encoding alone.
\textbf{components} gives one sentence with the graph's connected-component
count. \textbf{degree} states each node's exact degree; \textbf{clustering}
each node's clustering coefficient; \textbf{rwse} each node's random-walk
return probabilities \citep{dwivedi2022graph}. \textbf{all} combines
degree, clustering and rwse (not components). Mean rendered length at
$n{=}40$ ranges from $37$ characters (\texttt{components}) to $4{,}691$
(\texttt{all}); \texttt{filler} at $1{,}829$ characters brackets
\texttt{degree} ($891$) and \texttt{clustering} ($1{,}629$) from above but
sits below \texttt{rwse} ($2{,}949$) and \texttt{all}, so it bounds rather
than exactly matches the length term for those two conditions.

\subsection{Shortcut solvers}
\label{sec:shortcuts}

A primer's measured accuracy is evidence of graph reasoning only once it
exceeds what a solver that never sees the graph can already recover from
the primer text. Our shortcut solver combines sixteen exact rules, one
heuristic, eight fitted rules (fit and scored on structurally disjoint
graph sets), and an exact enumeration bound for small graphs; being
structurally incapable of reading the graph object, its accuracy on a
(task, condition) cell is a content-only lower bound, which we call the
\emph{shortcut bar} rather than a ``ceiling'' --- it never overstates what a
model could achieve, but does not itself upper-bound model accuracy.

The published GraphQA generator draws $5$--$19$-node graphs; our
evaluation corpus is $n{=}40$. A bar fit on the small-graph distribution
does not transfer: refitting the same solver on $n{=}40$
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs at the seven densities used in our sweep
(disjoint $300$-graph fit/test sets per density) moves the
\texttt{edge\_existence}/\texttt{none} bar from $0.498$ to a
density-averaged $0.735$, driven by class imbalance rather than anything a
primer supplies, and drops the \texttt{degree}-over-\texttt{none} gap on
that task from $+0.296$ to $+0.011$ --- below the threshold at which we
call a primer a substitute route. We therefore reclassify route status
using the $n{=}40$ refit (Table~\ref{tab:shortcut}), not the published
split: only \texttt{node\_degree} (under \texttt{rwse}, \texttt{degree},
\texttt{all}) and \texttt{edge\_count} (under \texttt{clustering},
\texttt{degree}, \texttt{all}) remain routes once bars are measured on the
right graph size; \texttt{edge\_existence} and \texttt{connected\_nodes} do
not. \texttt{node\_count} and \texttt{cycle\_check} are excluded from the
split entirely: at $n{=}40$ their gold answers are constant ($40$, and
``yes'' respectively, at every one of the seven densities we tested), so
every condition's bar sits near $1.0$ under \texttt{none} already, and the
published-split bars overstate what a primer adds here. The published-split
bars (Table~\ref{tab:shortcut}, top) remain the correct instrument for the
held-out arms of Section~\ref{sec:setup}, which answer that corpus.

\subsection{Rewiring: isolating structure from length}
\label{sec:rewiring}

The length-matched \texttt{filler} bounds the length confound but is not an
exact match (Section~\ref{sec:primers}), and no design that compares
different graphs (or different generators) can hold a structural quantity
fixed while claiming to vary only it: across five generator families at
matched $(n, m)$, the majority-class baseline alone ranges $0.15$ to
$1.000$. We add a within-instance design instead. A double-edge swap
removes edges $(a,b)$, $(c,d)$ and adds $(a,d)$, $(c,b)$
\citep{fosdick2018configuring}; every endpoint keeps its degree exactly, so
the degree sequence, $n$, $m$, and the rendered prompt's character length
are unchanged, while the triangle count --- what \texttt{clustering}
reports --- moves. We generate \texttt{low}/\texttt{base}/\texttt{high}
triangle-count graphs this way ($n{=}40$, $\bar k{=}12$, $200$ graphs per
level) and evaluate \texttt{node\_degree} under \texttt{none},
\texttt{clustering} and \texttt{filler} on each, on \texttt{qwen3-1.7b},
its thinking twin, and \texttt{qwen35-2b} (Section~\ref{sec:results-clustering}).

\subsection{Answer extraction and measurement validity}
\label{sec:extraction}

Turning free model text into a scored answer is a quiet source of
measurement error, and it is not symmetric across conditions: thinking
responses are longer and messier than zero-shot ones, so a naive extractor
makes the thinking arm look worse than it is on exactly the comparison we
want to draw. One failure is worth reporting in full because it produced a
headline-sized effect that did not exist. Our boolean extractor originally
took the last yes/no token in the response; on \texttt{cycle\_check},
\texttt{qwen3-4b} frequently answers correctly and then recites the
definition (``\dots a path with no repeated edges or nodes''), and the
trailing ``no'' became the extracted answer. Correcting the extractor to
ignore ``no repeated'' moved $225$ of \texttt{qwen3-4b}'s scored boolean
rows from wrong to right, all on \texttt{cycle\_check}, none the other
way; before the fix this read as a dramatic condition-dependent collapse,
after it nothing on that task is significant. All numbers here use the
corrected extractor. We also verified directly that Qwen3's \texttt{<think>}
tags never reach the extractor unstripped for the thinking arms (a spot
check of $50$ random rows per arm, plus a corpus-wide comparison of
extraction with and without the pre-\texttt{</think>} text, finds no
mismatch), and that scoring truncated (\texttt{hit\_cap}) generations as
wrong rather than dropping them changes the verdict on two cells (
\texttt{rwse}/\texttt{cycle\_check} and \texttt{clustering},
\texttt{all}/\texttt{cycle\_check} for \texttt{qwen3-1.7b}; full comparison
in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}). We drop truncated pairs throughout
the main text and report the alternative there.

\subsection{Significance testing}
\label{sec:prompts}

Every reported comparison is paired: a condition against \texttt{none} (or
another condition) on the same graph and question, truncated pairs dropped
from both sides. The primary test is the exact McNemar test
\citep{mcnemar1947note}, Benjamini--Hochberg corrected
\citep{benjaminihochberg1995controlling} within each (arm, task) family of
six conditions at $q{=}0.05$; where we report ``beats both controls'' we
additionally require significance under a \emph{global} BH correction
pooling every test run for that claim, not only the family. For the
baseline-dependence relation (Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}) we use a
cross-fitted design --- baseline estimated from one random half of a
cell's paired graphs, delta from the disjoint other half, both
directions pooled --- with a cluster bootstrap that resamples whole (arm,
task, density) blocks rather than individual cells, since conditions at the
same block share one \texttt{none} sample. For null cells we report a
minimum detectable effect (fast preset: $50$ replicates, $200$
permutations, $5$ power-curve steps), so an absence of significance is
distinguished from a demonstrated absence of effect rather than left
ambiguous.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks.} Six GraphQA tasks \citep{fatemi2023talklikeagraph}: node
count, edge count, node degree, connected nodes, edge existence, cycle
check.

\paragraph{Models.} We compare \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} and \texttt{qwen3-4b}
\citep{qwen2025qwen3}, each with native thinking mode on and off, for four
arms. This pair was chosen for its paired thinking/non-thinking checkpoints
at two sizes within one open-weight family and moderate compute cost; we
make no claim about an architectural advantage for graph tasks specifically,
and Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline} extends the comparison to eleven
further arms including a second family.

\paragraph{Sweep design.} All four arms answer one shared prompt set:
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs \citep{erdosrenyi1959random}, $n{=}40$, four
densities $p\in\{.10,.20,.35,.50\}$ (\texttt{densfull40}), $100$ graphs per
(density, task) cell across all seven conditions and six tasks --- $16{,}800$
prompts per arm. Generation is greedy; the output budget is $2{,}048$
tokens for the plain arms and $8{,}192$ for the thinking arms (the two are
not the same budget, and we say so wherever a comparison depends on it). A
second corpus (\texttt{densfull40hi}) extends \texttt{node\_degree} and
\texttt{edge\_existence} to $p\in\{.65,.75,.85\}$, $100$ graphs/cell, all
four arms. A dedicated \texttt{node\_degree} sweep at $400$ graphs/density
level (all seven densities, \texttt{qwen3-1.7b}) backs the one content
effect that survives every control (Section~\ref{sec:results-clustering}).

\paragraph{Held-out arms.} Eleven further arms were not used to develop any
claim here, though the corpus that eight of them answer --- the published
GraphQA split, $30$ graphs/cell, five tasks, six conditions, plus a rerun of
\texttt{filler} and \texttt{edge\_existence} after a wording fix --- predates
this project and was analysed earlier for other questions, so we call them
earlier-collected rather than pre-registered. Eight arms
(\texttt{qwen3-8b}, \texttt{qwen3-14b}, \texttt{gemma-4-E4B-it},
\texttt{gemma-4-12B-it}, each $\pm$ thinking) answer that split; three more
(\texttt{qwen3-0.6b} $\pm$ thinking, \texttt{qwen35-2b}) answer a
$100$-graph \texttt{\{none, degree\}} probe over all six tasks. Together
they extend the comparison to $0.6$--$14$B and two families
(Section~\ref{sec:results-heldout}).

\paragraph{Metrics.} Exact match for every task, including
\texttt{connected\_nodes}: its set-F1 is $0.96$--$1.00$ everywhere at this
graph size, so it does not discriminate between conditions and exact match
is what every table here uses.

\paragraph{Known validity caveats.} \texttt{node\_count} and
\texttt{cycle\_check} are shortcut-dominated at $n{=}40$
(Section~\ref{sec:shortcuts}); \texttt{degree} and \texttt{all} state the
\texttt{node\_degree} and \texttt{edge\_count} answers verbatim. Results on
these cells are reported but bound what can be concluded about graph
reasoning rather than demonstrate it.

\subsection{Length is an active treatment}
\label{sec:results-length}

\texttt{filler} is not inert. It significantly harms \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} on
\texttt{edge\_existence} ($-15.8$) and \texttt{connected\_nodes} ($-6.2$),
and \texttt{qwen3-4b} on both ($-7.0$, $-12.5$). Over the full seven-density
range (\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think}, \texttt{node\_degree}) it costs $-6.3$
points pooled ($p{<}10^{-4}$): negligible at $p{\le}.35$, then $-9.5$,
$-9.3$, $-14.1$, $-11.7$ at the four densest levels
($r{=}{+0.953}$, $p{=}0.00088$ against baseline). Length's cost grows with
task difficulty, the same direction an answer-stating primer's benefit
grows in (Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}); pooling all conditions into
one correlation against \texttt{none} would understate both.

The largest \texttt{edge\_existence} swings in Table~\ref{tab:main-results-plain}
are not graph reading. Table~\ref{tab:booleanbias} decomposes accuracy into
hit rate and false-alarm rate: hit rate sits at $99$--$100\%$ under every
condition for both arms, so the model essentially never misses a real
edge. What moves is the false-alarm rate --- how often it says ``yes'' to a
non-edge --- and it moves by condition, not by anything resembling graph
content: \texttt{filler} \emph{raises} it ($40.3{\to}61.8\%$, $12.6{\to}
22.5\%$) while \texttt{clustering} lowers it. We read \texttt{edge\_existence}
condition effects as shifting a yes/no response bias, not as evidence of
reading the graph more or less accurately.

\texttt{node\_count}'s largest apparent gains are the same kind of
artifact. Gold is the constant $40$ at this graph size; the dominant
\texttt{none} failure is the answer ``$39$'', an off-by-one from the
encoder's ``nodes $0,\dots,39$'' preamble. \texttt{qwen3-1.7b}'s ``$39$''
rate is $68.5\%$ under \texttt{none}, $61.8\%$ under \texttt{degree},
$28.8\%$ under \texttt{filler}, and $1.3\%$ under \texttt{clustering} ---
almost any interposed text reduces it, \texttt{clustering} nearly
eliminates it, and none of this is a structural primer effect on a task
with one possible answer.

Table~\ref{tab:baseline} splits the $354$ non-degenerate cells by whether
the shortcut solver, refit at $n{=}40$, recovers more from the condition
than from \texttt{none}. Where it does, effect falls with baseline at
$r{=}{-0.417}$ ($p{=}1.1{\times}10^{-4}$); where it does not, there is no
relation ($r{=}{-0.050}$, $p{=}0.41$). Because this split's two groups are
not baseline-matched, we treat Figure~\ref{fig:crossfit} --- baseline and
delta estimated from disjoint graph halves, so a low-baseline pair cannot
mechanically inflate its own delta --- as the stronger evidence: $r{=}
{-0.31}$ over $232$ route cells ($p{=}1.8{\times}10^{-6}$, $95\%$
cluster-bootstrap CI $[-0.42,-0.17]$) against $r{=}{+0.02}$ over $762$
no-route cells ($p{=}0.51$). A mixed-effects model with a random intercept
per arm gives the same sign and a comparable magnitude on the route cells
($-12.0$, $p{=}1.2{\times}10^{-5}$; only four arms, so we read this as a
consistency check, not an independently powered test).

The density continuum makes the same point with difficulty manipulated
rather than observed. Holding the arm (\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think}) and task
(\texttt{node\_degree}) fixed and varying only density from $p{=}.10$ to
$.85$ drives \texttt{none} accuracy from $0.95$ to $0.44$;
\texttt{degree}'s benefit rises from $+3.1$ to $+34.2$ points
($r{=}{-0.975}$, $p{=}1.9{\times}10^{-4}$), while \texttt{filler}'s cost
grows from negligible to $-14.1$ ($r{=}{+0.953}$, $p{=}8.8{\times}10^{-4}$).
The two effects run in opposite directions and grow together as the task
hardens.

\paragraph{Held-out arms and a second family.}
\label{sec:results-heldout}
On the eleven held-out arms (including, unlike an earlier pass, the
\texttt{.rerun} rows for \texttt{filler} and \texttt{edge\_existence}), the
relation reappears where a route exists: $r{=}{-0.674}$ over $105$ cells
($p{\approx}0$), against $r{=}{-0.186}$ over $93$ no-route cells
($p{=}0.075$). Regressing effect on baseline, $\log$ parameter count and
thinking mode together gives a baseline coefficient of $-30.2$ ($p{=}3.5
{\times}10^{-15}$) and a parameter coefficient of $+0.40$ ($p{=}0.54$):
once baseline is in the model, scale adds nothing measurable. Replacing
each cell's own baseline with the \emph{other} arms' accuracy on the same
cell --- a negative control sharing no observation with the effect ---
leaves no relation ($r{=}{+0.113}$, $p{=}0.135$), so what the effect tracks
is a model's own competence on an item, not a difficulty intrinsic to the
item.

\paragraph{Renaming nodes.} If the route effect were token copying rather
than content substitution, renaming nodes from integers to
Game-of-Thrones characters throughout the prompt should disrupt it. It does
not: on the published split, the route-offering \texttt{degree} condition's
effect size correlates $r{=}0.933$ between the integer- and GoT-named
sweeps ($39$ cells), while non-route conditions correlate weakly or not at
all (\texttt{clustering} $r{=}0.079$, \texttt{components} $r{=}0.345$,
\texttt{rwse} $r{=}{-0.381}$). Separately, renaming nodes with no primer at
all changes accuracy by a precise, non-significant $-0.19$ points pooled
across eight arms (zero of eight significant after BH), so the
route-transfer result is not naming changing accuracy on its own, but the
same substitution effect surviving a token-level change to how the answer
is expressed.

\paragraph{What copying looks like.} Where a primer states the answer and
the model still errs, $41.4\%$ of \texttt{qwen3-4b}'s $29$
\texttt{degree}/\texttt{node\_degree} errors state an adjacent node's
degree ($k{\pm}1$) rather than the queried node's, against $5.7\%$ under
\texttt{all}, which also states the answer but states thirty-nine other
facts alongside it. This is consistent with position-sensitive copying
under \texttt{degree} specifically, not a general degradation from stating
the answer.

\paragraph{Practical reading.} A primer effect reported against a
no-primer baseline, without reporting that baseline, is uninterpretable:
within \texttt{qwen3-4b} alone, \texttt{degree} is worth $+27.7$ on
\texttt{edge\_count} (baseline $0.02$) and $-6.5$ on \texttt{node\_degree}
(baseline $0.99$) --- a cross-task, not a same-task, contrast, since the
degree primer states \texttt{node\_degree}'s answer verbatim while only
implying \texttt{edge\_count}'s through a sum, but the direction is the
same one the baseline relation predicts.

measured off ceiling, at the baseline where it has room to show up.
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think} does not show it (Table~\ref{tab:main-think},

The mechanism we can rule in is limited. Figure~\ref{fig:rewiring} holds

(Section~\ref{sec:future-work}) rather than infer one.

\subsection{Thinking mode}
\label{sec:results-thinking}

Thinking moves most (task, arm) cells to ceiling, leaving no headroom for
a primer effect, and where it does not saturate a task it can censor it
instead (Table~\ref{tab:hitcap}). It also changes how long the model
generates before answering, which is itself condition-dependent:
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think}'s \texttt{degree} raises the non-termination rate
on \texttt{node\_degree} by $3.4$pp over \texttt{none} ($p{<}0.001$) while
\texttt{filler} lowers it; outside \texttt{edge\_count}, \texttt{degree}
produces $137$ non-terminating generations of which $7$ also fail to
terminate under \texttt{none} --- $130$ new failures attributable to the
primer. Separately, \texttt{qwen3-4b-think} answers correctly on all $29$
of the instances \texttt{degree} breaks for \texttt{qwen3-4b}, under the
identical primer and weights: whatever the primer disrupts is recoverable
within the same network once a reasoning trace runs, which we report as an
observation about the process, not as evidence for a specific mechanism.
We do not report a single pooled ``thinking beats every primer effect by
an order of magnitude'' figure: the comparisons available differ in token
budget ($2{,}048$ vs.\ $8{,}192$) and task mix, so a fair same-budget
comparison is not one we have.

\subsection{Earlier-collected models}
\label{sec:results-earlier}

The eleven held-out arms are mostly near ceiling under \texttt{none} on
the published split (mean $0.924$; \texttt{gemma4-12b} $0.989$,
\texttt{qwen3-8b-think} $1.000$), so headroom, not primer lift, is the
question they answer best; only the smallest arms
(\texttt{qwen3-0.6b} $0.705$, \texttt{qwen35-2b} $0.876$) have
meaningful room. A confirmatory $500$-graph replication
(\texttt{qwen3-8b}, \texttt{degree}, pre-specified condition) gives
$+6.67$pp pooled ($p{=}1{\times}10^{-4}$), one instance of route
substitution at $8$B. Renaming nodes to Game-of-Thrones characters with no
primer moves accuracy by $-0.19$pp pooled, zero of eight arms significant
after BH (Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}), a precise null over
$1{,}200{+}$ paired rows per arm.

\subsection{Power}
\label{sec:results-power}

For every null cell in the main sweep we compute a minimum detectable
effect (Section~\ref{sec:prompts}) rather than report only a $p$-value.
Coverage is uneven: on \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} and \texttt{qwen3-4b} $10$ of
$24$/$20$ null cells cannot detect even a $1.0$-point effect at our power
target; on \texttt{qwen3-4b-think}, near ceiling on most tasks, all $33$
null cells are underpowered at that threshold, which is the ceiling
speaking, not evidence the primer has no effect there. Full table in the
Appendix.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three regularities account for most of what we measure, and only one of
them is a content effect. First, a primer that states the requested
quantity acts as a second route to the answer, and whether that route
helps is set by whether it is more reliable than the model's own --- a
relation that holds across four cross-checks (cross-fitted cells, eleven
held-out arms, a negative-control difficulty estimate, and node renaming)
and needs none of them individually to be conclusive. Second, added text
is an active treatment regardless of content: the length-matched
\texttt{filler} harms both model sizes on some tasks, at magnitudes
exceeding most content effects measured here, and its cost grows with
task difficulty in the same way an answer-stating primer's benefit does.
An experimental design that compares a primer only against \texttt{none}
estimates content and length confounded. Third, headroom is scarce and
unevenly distributed: thinking saturates most cells, class imbalance
inflates \texttt{edge\_existence}'s blind-solver bar at this graph size,
and a fixed gold answer removes \texttt{node\_count} and
\texttt{cycle\_check} from consideration entirely.

Against that background, one content effect survives: \texttt{clustering}
on \texttt{node\_degree} beats a length control across most of the
density range in one model, and replicates in a second, larger model once
that model is evaluated off its own ceiling. We read this as a genuine
finding about where headroom and this content overlap, not as evidence for
a mechanism: the rewiring test that could show the benefit tracking
triangle count directly does not show that, and we report the negative
result alongside the positive one rather than the positive one alone.

We emphasise throughout that these are behavioural claims. We measured no
attention weights, activations or internal states, and did not intervene
on the model's computation.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The main sweep covers two model sizes from one family; the held-out arms
extend the range but on a smaller, earlier-collected corpus with fewer
conditions, and were not pre-registered against this project's hypotheses.
The route/no-route split depends on a threshold (Section~\ref{sec:shortcuts})
and on the $n{=}40$ shortcut refit's own fit/test split; we report the
reclassification it produced relative to the published-split bars as a
sensitivity check rather than treating either bar set as definitive.
Graphs are drawn from one generator family (Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi); the
rewiring test varies triangle count within that family but does not test
transfer to another. The length control (\texttt{filler}) brackets rather
than exactly matches length for two of five content conditions. The
crossover baseline at which an answer-stating primer stops helping does
not transfer between corpora (near $0.7$ at $n{=}40$ on the route-only
split, near $0.99$ on the published split), so it is a property of a
corpus, not a universal constant. Finally, several results in
Section~\ref{sec:results-clustering} and~\ref{sec:results-earlier} rest on
$n{\le}300$ per cell; we report a minimum detectable effect for every null
result but that does not substitute for a larger sample where one would
change the conclusion.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

Two prompt-only experiments would discriminate directly between the
accounts in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} without new machinery. A
\emph{single-fact} primer stating only the queried node's degree keeps the
answer available while removing the other $39$ sentences, separating
interference from route substitution. A \emph{corrupted} primer stating a
wrong degree measures reliance directly: a model that copies collapses, a
model that computes is unaffected. Extending the clustering-on-node\_degree
sweep and the rewiring test to \texttt{qwen3-4b} at high density, and down
and up the Qwen3 parameter ladder, would turn the two-point cross-scale
replication in Section~\ref{sec:results-clustering} into a curve. The
rewiring result itself invites a direct test of what \emph{does} predict
the clustering benefit, since triangle count alone does not appear to.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

This work evaluates open-weight language models on synthetically generated
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs. It involves no human subjects, no personal or
identifying data, and no annotation labour. We see no direct risk of harm
from the findings; the principal societal cost is the compute expended.
