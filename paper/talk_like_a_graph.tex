\begin{abstract}
Prepending a \emph{primer} of factual sentences about a graph's structure
(node degree, clustering coefficient, random-walk return probabilities or
component count) to its text encoding is a natural way to assist a
language model on graph-reasoning questions. An apparent gain is
uninformative if the answer can be read from the primer without consulting
the graph, and confounded if the primer merely lengthens the prompt. We
evaluate primers against four controls jointly: a graph-blind shortcut
solver refit at the evaluated graph size, a length-matched content-free
filler, a minimal-length content control, and a degree-preserving rewiring
that varies triangle count at fixed prompt length. Across four Qwen3 arms
(1.7B and 4B, with and without thinking mode) on $40$-node
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs, primers that state the answer help a model at
floor and harm a model at ceiling (cross-fitted interaction with baseline
accuracy $-11.0$ points, $95\%$ CI $[-17.1, -5.7]$), whereas primers that
do not state it show no such relation. The pattern replicates on eleven
held-out arms ($0.6$--$14$B, two families) but is not graded at
intermediate baselines. The only effect of a primer that does not state
the answer, \texttt{clustering} on \texttt{node\_degree}, is small relative
to no primer ($+1.9$ points) and confined to intermediate densities; two
candidate mechanisms for it are not supported.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph} show that a large language model's
accuracy on graph-reasoning questions varies substantially with the choice
of text encoding when the graph and question are held fixed. We ask a
complementary question about the same benchmark: does prepending a short
\emph{primer} of derived, node-level facts to the graph encoding improve
the model's answers?

Four confounds complicate the interpretation of such an effect. First, a
primer may help because the model reads the answer from it rather than
reasoning over the graph (answer leakage). Second, a primer changes prompt
length, and context length and position alone affect retrieval and
reasoning accuracy \citep{liu2024lostinthemiddle,kamradt2023needle,
levy2024sametask}. Third, answer extraction can err in ways that correlate
with response style, which appears as a spurious condition effect
(Section~\ref{sec:extraction}). Fourth, a model near ceiling on a task
leaves no room for any primer effect to appear.

We address three research questions. \textbf{RQ1}: when a primer improves
accuracy, is the improvement attributable to the primer stating the
answer? \textbf{RQ2}: does added text affect accuracy independently of its
content? \textbf{RQ3}: does any primer that does not state the answer
improve accuracy beyond a length-matched control, and through what
mechanism? Our contributions are (i) a battery of controls applied jointly:
a graph-blind shortcut solver refit at the evaluated graph size
(Section~\ref{sec:shortcuts}), a length-matched content-free
\texttt{filler}, a minimal-length content control (\texttt{components}),
and a degree-preserving rewiring that changes triangle count at fixed
prompt length (Section~\ref{sec:rewiring}); (ii) an empirical map of primer
effects on $40$-node Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs across four model arms and
six tasks; (iii) evidence that answer-stating primers act as a substitute
route whose value depends on the model's own baseline accuracy, supported
by a cross-fitted interaction test and replicated on held-out models and
under node renaming; and (iv) a characterisation of the single effect of a
primer that does not state the answer, together with two mechanism tests
that it fails.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

We adopt the GraphQA task definitions of \citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph}
and manipulate graph size and edge density directly, so that difficulty is
an experimental variable rather than an incidental one.
\citet{wang2023nlgraph} report that LLMs are brittle to spurious
correlations in graph problems and propose \emph{Build-a-Graph} and
\emph{Algorithmic} prompting, the prompt-level interventions closest to a
primer; \citet{perozzi2024letyourgraph} instead learn a continuous graph
encoding. \citet{guo2023gpt4graph} find that models struggle to exploit
graph description languages directly, and
\citet{jin2024largelanguagemodelsongraphs} survey the wider literature.
\citet{dziri2023faithandfate} argue that transformers solve compositional
tasks by linearised subgraph matching rather than systematic reasoning,
which raises the same question as a primer's benefit: does it reflect
reasoning or a shorter pattern to match? \citet{geirhos2020shortcut} frame
this as \emph{shortcut learning}; our shortcut solver operationalises that
concern for the primer channel. Irrelevant context degrades reasoning
\citep{shi2023distracted}, as does input length well below the context
limit \citep{levy2024sametask}; our \texttt{filler} condition measures this
cost directly. The \texttt{rwse} condition adapts the random-walk
structural encoding of \citet{dwivedi2022graph}, rendered as sentences, and
the rewiring follows the double-edge-swap configuration model
\citep{fosdick2018configuring}. For statistics we follow
\citet{dror2018hitchhikers} on paired testing, test differences between
groups directly rather than contrasting their significance
\citep{gelman2006difference}, and borrow cross-fitting from
\citet{chernozhukov2018double}. We distinguish Qwen3's native thinking mode,
toggled through the chat template, from chain-of-thought \emph{prompting}
\citep{wei2022chainofthought}; this paper tests only the former.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Primers}
\label{sec:primers}

A primer is a preamble of factual sentences about a graph's structure,
prepended to the standard text encoding and the task question (a complete
example appears in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-prompt}). A single renderer
produces all seven conditions, so conditions share sentence framing and
number formatting and differ only in the facts stated. The exception is
\textbf{filler}, which states no fact and therefore uses its own frame
(``Node~$N$ is simply present within the graph G.''); it is a length
control, not a content control. \textbf{none} is the graph encoding alone.
\textbf{components} gives one sentence stating the number of connected
components. \textbf{degree} states each node's degree, \textbf{clustering}
each node's clustering coefficient, and \textbf{rwse} each node's
random-walk return probabilities after two and three steps
\citep{dwivedi2022graph}. \textbf{all} combines degree, clustering and rwse.
Because the model reads tokens rather than characters, we measure length
with the Qwen3 tokenizer: mean primer length at $n{=}40$ is $8$ tokens
(\texttt{components}), $372$ (\texttt{degree}), $470$ (\texttt{filler}),
$510$ (\texttt{clustering}), $1{,}070$ (\texttt{rwse}) and $1{,}612$
(\texttt{all}), against a mean \texttt{none} prompt of $2{,}165$ tokens.
\texttt{filler} is therefore longer than \texttt{degree}, $8\%$ shorter than
\texttt{clustering} (a conservative control for it), and substantially
shorter than \texttt{rwse} and \texttt{all}, for which it bounds rather than
matches the length term.

\subsection{Shortcut solvers}
\label{sec:shortcuts}

A primer's measured benefit is evidence of graph reasoning only if it
exceeds what a solver that never sees the graph can recover from the
primer text. Our shortcut solver combines sixteen exact rules, one
heuristic, eight fitted rules (fit and scored on disjoint graph sets) and an
exact enumeration bound for small graphs. Because it has no access to the
graph object, its accuracy on a (task, condition) cell is a content-only
lower bound, which we call the \emph{shortcut bar}: it never overstates
what the primer text alone permits, but it does not upper-bound model
accuracy.

The published GraphQA generator draws graphs of $5$--$19$ nodes, whereas
our evaluation corpus has $n{=}40$, and a bar fit on small graphs does not
transfer. Refitting the solver on $n{=}40$ Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs at the
seven densities of our sweep (disjoint $300$-graph fit and test sets per
density) raises the \texttt{edge\_existence}/\texttt{none} bar from $0.498$
to $0.735$, an effect of class imbalance rather than primer content, and
reduces the \texttt{degree}-over-\texttt{none} gap on that task from
$+0.296$ to $+0.011$. We classify a (task, condition) pair as offering a
\emph{substitute route} when its bar exceeds the \texttt{none} bar by more
than $0.05$; at $n{=}40$ every gain is either at most $0.011$ or at least
$0.066$, so any threshold between these values yields the same split. Under
the refit bars (Table~\ref{tab:shortcut}, bottom), only
\texttt{node\_degree} (under \texttt{rwse}, \texttt{degree}, \texttt{all})
and \texttt{edge\_count} (under \texttt{clustering}, \texttt{degree},
\texttt{all}) offer a route. \texttt{node\_count} and \texttt{cycle\_check}
are excluded from the split: at $n{=}40$ their gold answers are constant
($40$, and ``yes'' at all seven densities), so a blind solver reaches
$1.00$ under every condition. The published-split bars
(Table~\ref{tab:shortcut}, top) are used for the held-out arms, which
answer that corpus.

\subsection{Rewiring: isolating structure from length}
\label{sec:rewiring}

The \texttt{filler} condition bounds the length confound only approximately
(Section~\ref{sec:primers}), and comparisons across graph generators cannot
isolate a structural quantity: across five generator families at matched
$(n, m)$, the majority-class baseline alone ranges from $0.15$ to $1.00$. We
therefore use a within-instance design. A double-edge swap removes edges
$(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$ and adds $(a,d)$ and $(c,b)$
\citep{fosdick2018configuring}; every endpoint keeps its degree, so the
degree sequence, $n$, $m$, the gold answer and the prompt length are
unchanged, while the triangle count, which the \texttt{clustering} primer
reports, changes. We generate \texttt{low}, \texttt{base} and \texttt{high}
triangle-count variants of each graph ($n{=}40$, mean degree $12$, $200$
graphs per level; mean clustering coefficient $0.01$, $0.31$ and $0.73$)
and evaluate \texttt{node\_degree} under \texttt{none}, \texttt{clustering}
and \texttt{filler} with \texttt{qwen3-1.7b}, \texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think} and
\texttt{qwen35-2b} (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

The design tests two predictions. If the \texttt{clustering} benefit
derives from the stated values, it should vary with triangle count when
everything else is fixed. More specifically, local clustering in an
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graph is approximately the edge probability $p$, so the
primer implicitly states the density, and $\bar{C}\,(n-1)$ approximates the
expected degree. A model that used the primer as a density prior would bias
its degree estimates toward $\bar{C}\,(n-1)$, which equals $0.5$, $12.0$ and
$28.7$ across the three levels while the true mean degree stays at $12$.

\subsection{Answer extraction and measurement validity}
\label{sec:extraction}

Answer extraction errs asymmetrically across conditions, because thinking
responses are longer and less regular than direct ones. One failure
produced a spurious effect of headline magnitude: the boolean extractor
took the last yes/no token, and on \texttt{cycle\_check}
\texttt{qwen3-4b} often answers correctly and then recites the definition
of a cycle (``\dots a path with no repeated edges or nodes''). Ignoring ``no
repeated'' moved $225$ of \texttt{qwen3-4b}'s rows from incorrect to
correct and none in the other direction, and removed an apparent
condition-dependent collapse; all results use the corrected extractor. A
manual check of $50$ rows per arm and a corpus-wide comparison found that
\texttt{<think>} blocks never reach the extractor unstripped. Scoring
truncated generations as incorrect instead of dropping them changes the
significance verdict on three \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} \texttt{cycle\_check}
cells (\texttt{rwse}, \texttt{clustering}, \texttt{all};
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}); the main text drops truncated pairs.

\subsection{Significance testing}
\label{sec:significance}

Every comparison is paired: a condition against \texttt{none} (or another
condition) on the same graph and question, with pairs dropped when either
side is truncated. The primary test is the exact McNemar test
\citep{mcnemar1947note}, Benjamini--Hochberg corrected
\citep{benjaminihochberg1995controlling} within each (arm, task) family of
six conditions at $q{=}0.05$; where we claim that a condition beats both
controls, we additionally require significance under a global correction
across every test run for that claim. For the relation between effect and
baseline (Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}), baseline and effect are
estimated from disjoint random halves of each cell's paired graphs, and the
difference between route and no-route cells is tested directly as the
interaction in $\Delta \sim \mathrm{baseline} \times \mathrm{route}$, with a
bootstrap that resamples whole (arm, task, density) blocks, because
conditions within a block share one \texttt{none} sample. For each null cell
we estimate a minimum detectable effect (MDE): the smallest paired effect
that the same test detects with probability $0.8$ at $\alpha{=}0.05$ in
simulation ($50$ replicates, $200$ permutations per replicate).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks.} Six GraphQA tasks \citep{fatemi2023talklikeagraph}: node
count, edge count, node degree, connected nodes, edge existence and cycle
check.

\paragraph{Models.} An \emph{arm} is a model checkpoint with a fixed
decoding configuration. The main arms are Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B
\citep{qwen2025qwen3}, each with native thinking mode off
(\texttt{qwen3-1.7b}, \texttt{qwen3-4b}) and on (\texttt{-think}), chosen
because they offer both modes of the same weights at two sizes and
moderate cost.

\paragraph{Sweep design.} All four arms answer one shared prompt set:
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs \citep{erdosrenyi1959random} with $n{=}40$ at
densities $p\in\{.10,.20,.35,.50\}$, $100$ graphs per (density, task) cell,
seven conditions and six tasks, for $16{,}800$ prompts per arm. Decoding is
greedy with a budget of $2{,}048$ new tokens for plain arms and $8{,}192$
for thinking arms; Section~\ref{sec:results-thinking} reports a
budget-matched comparison. A high-density extension covers
\texttt{node\_degree} and \texttt{edge\_existence} at $p\in\{.65,.75,.85\}$
($100$ graphs per cell, all four arms). For the one content effect that
survives the controls (Section~\ref{sec:results-clustering}), a dedicated
\texttt{node\_degree} sweep with \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} uses $400$ graphs per
density at all seven densities, and a replication uses $400$ newly seeded
graphs per density at $p\le.50$.

\paragraph{Held-out arms.} Eleven further arms were not used to develop any
claim reported here. Eight (Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, Gemma-4-E4B-it and
Gemma-4-12B-it \citep{gemma4}, each with and without thinking) answer the
published GraphQA split ($30$ graphs per cell, five tasks, six conditions);
three (Qwen3-0.6B with and without thinking, and Qwen3.5-2B
\citep{qwen35}, \texttt{qwen35-2b}) answer a $100$-graph probe of
\texttt{none} and \texttt{degree} on all six tasks. The published-split
responses were collected before this project's hypotheses were formed and
were analysed earlier for other questions, so we describe these arms as
held out rather than pre-registered.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Exact match for every task. For \texttt{connected\_nodes},
set-F1 is $0.96$--$1.00$ in every cell at this graph size and does not
discriminate between conditions, so we report exact match. For the integer
tasks, mean absolute error is reported in the Appendix.

\subsection{Length is an active treatment}
\label{sec:results-length}

\texttt{filler} is not inert. It significantly reduces accuracy for
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b} on \texttt{edge\_existence} ($-15.8$) and
\texttt{connected\_nodes} ($-6.2$), and for \texttt{qwen3-4b} on both
($-7.0$, $-12.5$). Over the full seven-density range
(\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think}, \texttt{node\_degree}) it costs $6.3$ points
pooled ($p{<}10^{-4}$): negligibly at $p{\le}.35$, then $9.5$, $9.3$, $14.1$
and $11.7$ points at the four densest levels ($r{=}{+0.953}$ with
baseline across levels, $p{=}0.00088$). The cost of added length thus grows
with task difficulty, in the same direction as the benefit of an
answer-stating primer (Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}).

The largest \texttt{edge\_existence} effects in
Table~\ref{tab:main-results-plain} do not reflect more accurate reading of
the graph. Table~\ref{tab:booleanbias} decomposes accuracy into hit rate and
false-alarm rate: the hit rate is $99$--$100\%$ under every condition for
both arms, so accuracy varies only through false alarms, which
\texttt{filler} increases ($40.3{\to}61.8\%$ and $12.6{\to}22.5\%$) and
\texttt{clustering} decreases. A signal-detection analysis
\citep{macmillan2005detection}, with the log-linear correction for extreme
proportions \citep{hautus1995corrections}, cannot separate a shift in
response criterion from a change in sensitivity here: with the hit rate at
ceiling, a change in the false-alarm rate alone moves $d'$ and $c$
together and cannot be attributed to either (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:sdt}). We therefore report only that accuracy on this task
moves through the false-alarm rate, and do not interpret these effects as
changes in graph reading. Under \texttt{none}, \texttt{qwen3-1.7b}'s
criterion becomes steadily more liberal with density ($c$ from $-0.27$ at
$p{=}.10$ to $-1.74$ at $p{=}.50$).

\texttt{node\_count}'s largest apparent gains are also an artifact. Gold is
the constant $40$; the dominant \texttt{none} error is ``$39$'', an
off-by-one induced by the encoder's ``nodes $0,\dots,39$'' preamble.
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b} answers ``$39$'' on $68.5\%$ of instances under
\texttt{none}, $61.8\%$ under \texttt{degree}, $28.8\%$ under
\texttt{filler} and $1.3\%$ under \texttt{clustering}. Almost any
interposed text reduces this error, and none of these changes is a
structural effect on a task with a single possible answer.

Mean baseline is $0.61$ for route cells and $0.85$ for no-route cells, so

\label{tab:baseline}

A \emph{cell} is an (arm, task, density, condition) combination with at
least ten usable pairs. Table~\ref{tab:baseline} splits the $354$
main-sweep cells on non-constant tasks by whether the primer offers a
substitute route. Route cells gain at low baseline and lose at high
baseline ($r{=}{-0.417}$, $81$ cells); no-route cells do not
($r{=}{-0.050}$, $273$ cells). Two problems prevent reading this contrast
directly: baseline and effect share sampling noise, and a difference in
significance is not a significant difference \citep{gelman2006difference}.
We therefore estimate baseline and effect from disjoint halves of each
cell's graphs and test the route $\times$ baseline interaction, adding the
high-density extension ($117$ route and $381$ no-route cells;
Figure~\ref{fig:crossfit}). The interaction is $-11.0$ points per unit of
baseline ($95\%$ block-bootstrap CI $[-17.1, -5.7]$, $83$ blocks). Within
groups, the cross-fitted correlation is $r{=}{-0.34}$ for route cells
($116$ cells, one fold direction, $p{=}1.6{\times}10^{-4}$) and $r{=}{+0.03}$
for no-route cells; the other fold direction gives $-0.28$ and $+0.02$.

Accuracy is bounded, so a cell near $1.0$ can only lose and a cell near
$0.0$ can only gain. On the logit scale, which removes this compression,
the interaction persists ($-1.46$ logits, CI $[-2.11, -0.79]$). Restricted
to cells with baseline between $0.10$ and $0.90$, however, neither group
shows a relation (route $r{=}{-0.01}$, $78$ cells; no-route $r{=}{-0.07}$,
$187$ cells; interaction CI $[-29, +41]$). The evidence therefore supports a
contrast between floor and ceiling (a stated answer helps a model that
cannot compute it and harms one that already can) rather than a graded
dependence that can be detected at intermediate baselines, where our data
are sparsest (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:robust}).

The density continuum manipulates difficulty instead of observing it.
Holding the arm (\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think}) and task (\texttt{node\_degree})
fixed and raising density from $p{=}.10$ to $.85$ lowers \texttt{none}
accuracy from $0.95$ to $0.44$, while \texttt{degree}'s benefit rises from
$+3.1$ to $+34.2$ points ($r{=}{-0.975}$ across levels,
$p{=}1.9{\times}10^{-4}$) and \texttt{filler}'s cost grows from negligible
to $14.1$ points. The two effects run in opposite directions and both grow
as the task becomes harder.

\paragraph{Held-out arms and a second family.}
\label{sec:results-heldout}
On the eleven held-out arms the relation reappears where a route exists:
$r{=}{-0.674}$ over $105$ route cells ($p{=}3.5{\times}10^{-15}$) against
$r{=}{-0.186}$ over $93$ no-route cells ($p{=}0.075$), with an interaction
of $-24.7$ (block-bootstrap CI $[-109, -3.8]$, $p{=}0.027$, $43$ (arm, task)
blocks), weaker than on the main sweep but excluding zero. In an ordinary
least-squares regression of effect on baseline, log parameter count and
thinking mode (classical standard errors, so descriptive only), the
baseline coefficient is $-30.2$ and the parameter coefficient $+0.40$
($p{=}0.54$): once baseline is accounted for, scale adds nothing
measurable. Replacing each cell's own baseline with the other arms'
accuracy on the same cell, a negative control that shares no observation
with the effect, removes the relation ($r{=}{+0.113}$, $p{=}0.135$), so the
effect tracks a model's own competence rather than item difficulty. Most
held-out arms are near ceiling under \texttt{none} (mean $0.924$), and a
confirmatory $500$-graph run with a pre-specified condition
(\texttt{qwen3-8b}, \texttt{degree}) gives $+6.67$ points
($p{=}1{\times}10^{-4}$).

\paragraph{Renaming nodes.} Replacing integer node identifiers with
Game-of-Thrones character names throughout the prompt leaves the route
effect largely intact: on the published split, \texttt{degree}'s effect
correlates at $r{=}0.933$ between the integer- and name-based sweeps ($39$
cells), whereas non-route conditions correlate weakly or not at all
(\texttt{clustering} $r{=}0.079$, \texttt{components} $r{=}0.345$,
\texttt{rwse} $r{=}{-0.381}$). Renaming without a primer changes accuracy
by $-0.19$ points pooled across eight arms (none significant after
correction). Because renaming changes identifiers but not the stated degree
values, this shows that the effect does not depend on integer labels; it
does not distinguish copying the stated value from computing the answer,
which requires a primer that states incorrect values
(Section~\ref{sec:future-work}).

\paragraph{Error analysis.} When a primer states the answer and the model
still errs, the errors indicate positional copying. Of \texttt{qwen3-4b}'s
$29$ errors under \texttt{degree} on \texttt{node\_degree}, $12$ ($41.4\%$)
equal the stated degree of the node listed immediately before or after the
queried one, against $17.3\%$ expected by chance (binomial $p{=}.002$); under
\texttt{all}, which states the same degree amid three facts per node, the
rate is $2$ of $35$ ($5.7\%$).

The rewiring experiment constrains the mechanism but does not identify it
(Figure~\ref{fig:rewiring}). Mean clustering rises from $0.01$ to $0.31$
to $0.73$ across levels by construction, but the benefit does not follow:
for \texttt{qwen3-1.7b}, \texttt{clustering} minus \texttt{none} is $+3.5$,
$+6.0$ and $+2.5$ points at the three levels, broadly parallel to
\texttt{none}'s own level-to-level variation, and for \texttt{qwen35-2b}
\texttt{filler} matches or exceeds \texttt{clustering} at every level. The
density-prior prediction (Section~\ref{sec:rewiring}) fails as well: it
implies that errors under \texttt{clustering} should shift upward by up to
$17$ at the \texttt{high} level, but the paired shift in signed error
relative to \texttt{none} is within $0.3$ of zero at every level for all
three arms (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:prior}). Neither mechanism we can test
accounts for the effect.

\subsection{Thinking mode}
\label{sec:results-thinking}

Thinking brings most cells to ceiling, and where it does not, truncation
censors the task (Table~\ref{tab:hitcap}). Generation length also depends on
condition: for \texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think}, \texttt{degree} raises the
non-termination rate on \texttt{node\_degree} by $3.4$ points
($p{<}0.001$), producing $130$ non-terminating generations outside
\texttt{edge\_count} that do not occur under \texttt{none}.
\texttt{qwen3-4b-think} answers correctly on all $29$ instances on which
\texttt{degree} causes \texttt{qwen3-4b} to fail.

Because the two modes had different budgets, we rescored the thinking arms
as if capped at the plain arms' $2{,}048$ tokens; under greedy decoding this
is exact, since a trace that finished within $2{,}048$ tokens is unaffected
by the lower cap. At equal budget, thinking still outperforms the plain
mode on \texttt{edge\_existence} ($97.8$ vs.\ $70.5\%$ for 1.7B; $99.8$
vs.\ $90.5\%$ for 4B) and \texttt{node\_count}, but underperforms on
\texttt{cycle\_check} ($38.0$ vs.\ $90.7\%$; $62.0$ vs.\ $99.0\%$) and on
\texttt{node\_degree} for 4B ($91.2$ vs.\ $99.3\%$), and no
\texttt{edge\_count} trace finishes within the budget (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:budget}). Part of the advantage of thinking is therefore an
advantage of budget.

\subsection{Statistical power}
\label{sec:results-power}

For every null cell in the main sweep we estimate an MDE
(Section~\ref{sec:significance}). Where it is finite, the median MDE is
$5.6$ (\texttt{qwen3-1.7b}) and $10.0$ points (\texttt{qwen3-4b}) for gains
and $2$--$3$ points for losses. In $10$ of $24$ null cells for
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b}, $10$ of $20$ for \texttt{qwen3-4b}, $23$ of $33$ for
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think} and $33$ of $33$ for \texttt{qwen3-4b-think}, an
effect in at least one direction is undetectable at any magnitude because
\texttt{none} accuracy is at or near $0$ or $100\%$. These nulls reflect
ceiling or floor, not evidence of no effect.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three regularities account for most of the measured effects. For RQ1, a
primer that states the requested quantity acts as a second route to the
answer, helping a model at floor and harming one at ceiling; the contrast
survives cross-fitting, a logit transformation, held-out arms, a
negative-control difficulty estimate and node renaming, but it is not
graded at intermediate baselines, and renaming cannot establish whether the
model copies or computes. For RQ2, added text is an active treatment whose
cost exceeds most content effects and grows with difficulty, so a primer
compared only against \texttt{none} confounds content with length. Third,
headroom is scarce: thinking saturates most cells, and constant gold
answers remove two tasks from consideration. For RQ3, one effect of a
primer that does not state the answer survives, \texttt{clustering} on
\texttt{node\_degree}, worth four to seven points at intermediate densities
for \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} and present in \texttt{qwen3-4b} below ceiling, but
neither mechanism we could test explains it. All claims are behavioural: we
measured no internal states of the models.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

Each of the following needs new generations. A \emph{shuffled-value}
primer (same template and values, permuted across nodes) is a stricter
placebo than \texttt{filler}; a \emph{corrupted} primer stating wrong
degrees separates copying from computation; and a \emph{single-fact}
primer separates interference from route substitution. Varying primer
position, encoding and generator family (e.g.\ Barab\'asi--Albert, in which
clustering varies across nodes), matching fillers in tokens to each
condition, running the plain arms at $8{,}192$ tokens, and extending the
\texttt{clustering} sweep across Qwen3 sizes would address the remaining
limitations.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Structural primers change LLM accuracy on GraphQA mainly by stating the
answer, which helps only a model that cannot otherwise produce it, and by
lengthening the prompt, which costs accuracy on harder instances; the one
surviving effect of non-answer content is small and unexplained. Studies of
prompt-level graph augmentations should therefore report baseline
accuracy, a length-matched control and a graph-blind solver, and check for
constant answers at the evaluated graph size.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The main sweep covers two model sizes from one family; the held-out arms
extend the range but on a smaller corpus with fewer conditions and were not
pre-registered. The route classification depends on a threshold and on the
$n{=}40$ shortcut refit, and the route relation is not detectable within
the $0.10$--$0.90$ baseline window, where data are sparse. All graphs are
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi, in which local clustering is nearly uniform, so the
\texttt{clustering} primer carries little node-specific information; the
rewiring varies triangle count within this family but does not test other
families. The length control matches \texttt{clustering} to within $8\%$ in
tokens but is much shorter than \texttt{rwse} and \texttt{all}. Every prompt
uses one encoding (\texttt{incident}), one template and one primer
position, with greedy decoding. The \texttt{clustering} cell was selected
after the main sweep, and several results rest on $400$ or fewer instances
per cell; an MDE is reported for every null result, but it does not replace
a larger sample.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

This work evaluates open-weight language models on synthetically generated
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs. It involves no human subjects, no personal or
identifying data and no annotation labour. We see no direct risk of harm
from the findings; the principal cost is the GPU compute for approximately
$200{,}000$ generations.
