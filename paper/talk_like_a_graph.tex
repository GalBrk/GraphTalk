\begin{abstract}
Prepending a short \emph{primer} of factual sentences about each node's
local structure --- degree, clustering coefficient, a random-walk
structural encoding, or connected-component membership --- before a
graph's standard text encoding is an intuitively appealing way to ease
graph-reasoning prompts for a large language model. Any apparent gain is
illusory, however, if a solver can recover the answer directly from the
primer text without ever consulting the graph, and it is confounded if the
primer merely lengthens the prompt. We introduce a graph-blind shortcut
solver as a content-only lower bound and a length-matched filler control as
a length-only upper bound. Evaluated at $n=40$ on
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b} and \texttt{qwen3-4b}, with and without
chain-of-thought, we find that no primer effect replicates across model
scale. Across four arms, six tasks and six primers, several cells beat both
\texttt{none} and \texttt{filler} significantly in one model size, but none
does so in both. The largest consistent content effect --- the
\texttt{degree} primer on \texttt{node\_degree}, which states the answer
verbatim --- reverses sign between 1.7B ($+7.5$ points) and 4B ($-6.5$).
Nor is the filler inert: it costs the thinking arm up to $14.1$ points on
dense graphs and harms both sizes on \texttt{edge\_existence}. The largest
apparent gains fall on tasks whose answer is effectively constant, where
content-free text moves accuracy as much as a structural primer.
Chain-of-thought on an identical checkpoint is worth $+29.2$ points pooled,
an order of magnitude more than any primer effect measured. One cell
survives every control: \texttt{clustering} on \texttt{node\_degree} for
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b}, $+3.8$ points, replicated at $+4.3$
($p=0.0006$).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph} demonstrate that a large language model's
accuracy on graph-reasoning questions depends substantially on how the
graph is encoded as text, independent of its underlying reasoning ability.
Their study varies the encoding function --- adjacency lists, incident
lists, and ``themed'' encodings renaming nodes as people or places ---
while holding question and graph fixed.

This work poses a complementary question: rather than altering how edges
are described, does prepending a short \emph{primer} of derived,
node-level facts before the graph encoding help a model answer questions
about that graph? A primer lies outside the encoding vocabulary studied by
\citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph}: it is additional, redundant information
computed from the graph and expressed in plain sentences. Two confounds
make such an effect hard to read. If a primer helps because the model reads
the answer out of the primer rather than reasoning over the graph, that is
answer leakage, not reasoning. And if it helps or hurts because it
lengthens the prompt, that says nothing about its \emph{content}:
\citet{liu2024lostinthemiddle} and the needle-in-a-haystack protocol of
\citet{kamradt2023needle} show that context position and length alone move
retrieval accuracy, whatever the added text says.

This paper makes two contributions. First, a single, deterministic primer
renderer producing seven conditions (Section~\ref{sec:primers}) that differ
only in content, so differences between them are content effects rather
than formatting artifacts --- among them a length-matched,
information-free \texttt{filler} condition bounding the length term.
Second, a \emph{shortcut solver} (Section~\ref{sec:shortcuts}) answering
each GraphQA task from rendered primer text alone, with no access to the
graph, which lower-bounds what a primer gives away. We evaluate both in a
density sweep on \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} and \texttt{qwen3-4b}
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph} introduce GraphQA, a benchmark of graph
reasoning tasks paired with a family of graph-to-text encoding functions,
and show that encoding choice, task, and the underlying graph generator all
materially affect LLM accuracy. We adopt their task definitions, and their
finding that the generator matters motivates fixing graph size and edge
density explicitly (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) rather than sampling structure
incidentally.

A broader literature probes LLM graph reasoning.
\citet{wang2023nlgraph} report that LLMs are brittle to spurious
correlations in graph and problem structure; \citet{guo2023gpt4graph} find
that models struggle to exploit graph description languages directly; and
\citet{jin2024largelanguagemodelsongraphs} survey the wider space of
LLM-graph integration. \citet{dziri2023faithandfate} argue that
transformers solve compositional tasks by linearized subgraph matching
rather than systematic multi-step reasoning, which raises the question a
primer's apparent benefit also raises: is a gain evidence of reasoning, or
of a shorter pattern to match against? \citet{geirhos2020shortcut} frame
this generally as \emph{shortcut learning} --- a rule that performs well on
the benchmark without solving the intended problem. Our shortcut solver
(Section~\ref{sec:shortcuts}) operationalizes that concern for the primer
channel specifically, rather than for the model as a whole.

The RWSE condition adapts the random-walk structural encoding of
\citet{dwivedi2022graph}, developed for graph transformers; we render its
return probabilities as sentences rather than feeding them to an attention
mechanism. The chain-of-thought arm follows \citet{wei2022chainofthought},
and our paired testing follows \citet{dror2018hitchhikers}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Primers}
\label{sec:primers}

A primer is a short preamble of factual sentences describing a graph's
local or global structure, prepended to the standard text encoding and the
task question. A single renderer produces every condition, so all
node-level facts share identical sentence framing (``Node $N$ has\dots'')
and floating-point values are rounded and formatted identically; conditions
therefore differ in content alone. Six state a fact: \textbf{none}, the
encoding with no primer; \textbf{components}, one graph-level sentence
giving the number of connected components; \textbf{degree}, each node's
exact degree; \textbf{clustering}, each node's clustering coefficient;
\textbf{rwse}, each node's random-walk return probabilities
\citep{dwivedi2022graph}; and \textbf{all}, the latter three combined. The
seventh, \textbf{filler}, is a content-free control of comparable length
(``Node $N$ is simply present within the graph $G$.''). It isolates content
from the confound of merely lengthening the prompt.

\subsection{Shortcut solvers}
\label{sec:shortcuts}

For a primer's measured accuracy to constitute evidence of graph
reasoning, it must exceed what is recoverable from the primer text alone.
We construct a shortcut solver taking only rendered primer text as input
--- never the graph object, never the full-precision statistics the primer
rounds away --- and answering each GraphQA task directly. It combines
sixteen exact rules (deriving edge count algebraically from a
\texttt{degree} primer, for instance), one heuristic rule, eight fitted
rules whose fitting and scoring graph sets are kept structurally disjoint,
and an exact enumeration bound for small graphs. Being structurally
incapable of observing the graph, its accuracy on a (primer, task) cell is
a model-independent bound. Results there should be read against this
\emph{shortcut ceiling} rather than against zero. Where a primer fully
determines the answer --- a ceiling of $1.0$ --- model accuracy on that
cell is uninformative about graph reasoning, however high.

\subsection{Prompt assembly}
\label{sec:prompts}

Each prompt concatenates the primer, the graph's text encoding, and the
task question. Prompts are rebuilt from a canonicalized, parsed
representation of each graph rather than reused verbatim from the source
dataset, so node and edge ordering is reproducible and independent of the
order the original question happened to list them in.

\subsection{Significance testing}
\label{sec:significance}

Every comparison reported here is paired: a primer condition is compared
against \texttt{none} on the same graph, with the same question and gold
answer. Pairs in which either arm reached the output-token cap are dropped
from that comparison rather than scored as incorrect. Because a
graph contributes exactly one pair to a given (task, condition)
comparison, the pairs entering a single test are independent and no
clustering correction is required; we do not pool across tasks, where they
would not be \citep{dror2018hitchhikers}.

The primary test is the exact McNemar test \citep{mcnemar1947note},
Benjamini--Hochberg corrected across the six primer conditions within a
task \citep{benjaminihochberg1995controlling} --- $q=0.05$, six hypotheses
per family, not one family of $144$. For the comparisons we interpret
substantively we additionally report two resampling statistics: a paired
permutation test, sign-flipping each pair's observed difference over
$20{,}000$ resamples with the add-one correction of North et al., and a
paired bootstrap $95\%$ confidence interval on the effect size, resampling
whole pairs $20{,}000$ times. The permutation and McNemar $p$-values agree
to within a few thousandths on every cell where we report both, so the
interval, not the second $p$-value, is what the extra computation buys.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks.} We evaluate on six GraphQA tasks from
\citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph}: node count, edge count, node degree,
connected nodes, edge existence, and cycle check.

\paragraph{Models.} We compare \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} and \texttt{qwen3-4b}
from the Qwen3 model family \citep{qwen2025qwen3}, each evaluated in a
non-thinking (greedy decoding, no chain-of-thought) and a thinking
(chain-of-thought-enabled; \citealp{wei2022chainofthought}) variant, for
four model arms in total.

\paragraph{Sweep design.} All four arms are evaluated on one shared set of
prompts, so that every model receives byte-identical inputs. Graphs are
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi \citep{erdosrenyi1959random} with a fixed node count of
40 and edge density swept over four levels ($p \in \{0.10, 0.20, 0.35,
0.50\}$). Every combination of the seven primer conditions
(Section~\ref{sec:primers}) and six tasks is evaluated on 100 graphs per
(density, task) cell, yielding 16{,}800 prompts per model arm. Generation
is greedy, with a raised output budget (up to 8192 tokens), since at the
densest level tasks such as edge count require enumerating several hundred
edges in the answer.

\paragraph{Auxiliary sweeps.} Two comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:results}
need more power or a wider density range than the main sweep provides, and
use dedicated prompt sets built by the same renderer. The first fixes
\texttt{node\_degree} at $400$ graphs per density level over the same four
densities, run twice on independently drawn samples. The second extends the
range to $p \in \{0.10, \dots, 0.85\}$ on \texttt{node\_degree}, where the
main sweep's ceiling at $p \le 0.50$ leaves too little dynamic range. Both
are identified explicitly wherever their numbers appear.

\paragraph{Metrics and reproducibility.} We use exact match for integer-
and boolean-valued tasks and set-F1 for connected nodes, reporting every
result against both \texttt{none} and the corresponding shortcut ceiling.
Generations reaching the output-token cap are excluded from scoring rather
than scored incorrect, and reported separately as a per-cell drop count.
All generation is greedy, so a run is deterministic given a checkpoint and
a prompt file; thinking and non-thinking arms are the same checkpoints with
the model's native reasoning channel pinned on or off, not a prompt-level
instruction, so they differ in decoding mode alone. Code, prompt sets and
raw generations are released with the paper.

\paragraph{Known validity caveats.} Several cells are shortcut-dominated at
this graph size rather than genuine tests of graph reasoning: any primer
with one sentence per node lets node count be recovered by counting
sentences; cycle check's gold answer is ``yes'' for nearly every graph past
the sparsest level; and \texttt{degree} states the node-degree answer
verbatim. The one cell established as free of this contamination is node
degree under \texttt{\{none, components, clustering, filler\}}. We report
the contaminated cells with this caveat rather than omitting them, since a
null or negative result on an uncontaminated cell remains informative.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All four arms completed the sweep: $16{,}800$ scored generations each.
Unless stated otherwise, every reported $\Delta$ is a paired difference
against \texttt{none} over instances where neither arm truncated, tested
with an exact McNemar test \citep{mcnemar1947note} and Benjamini--Hochberg
corrected across the six primers within a task
\citep{benjaminihochberg1995controlling} at $q=0.05$.

\paragraph{Truncation must be read before accuracy.}
\label{sec:results-truncation}

The truncation rate is neither uniform across tasks nor across arms, and it
is not ignorable: on \texttt{edge\_count} the thinking arms truncate on
$62$--$81\%$ of instances, with individual (density, primer) cells at
$100\%$. Because truncated generations are dropped rather than scored
incorrect, the survivors there are a biased subset of the sparsest graphs,
and the apparently high \texttt{none} accuracy ($86.3\%$ at 1.7B, $97.6\%$
at 4B, against $2\%$ in the untruncated plain arms) measures which graphs
finished, not which were solved. We report \texttt{edge\_count} under
chain-of-thought but decline to interpret it; several of its comparisons
rest on fewer than $60$ surviving pairs.

The choice to drop rather than zero-score truncated rows is itself
consequential and we state it explicitly: scoring them as incorrect
reverses the sign of the \texttt{rwse} effect on 1.7B
\texttt{cycle\_check}, because \texttt{none} truncates more often ($9\%$)
than either \texttt{clustering} or \texttt{all} ($<1\%$) on that task.
Neither convention is uniformly more conservative.

\paragraph{No primer effect replicates across model scale.} Several
(primer, task) cells significantly exceed both \texttt{none} and
\texttt{filler} in one plain arm; none does so in both. The strongest
candidate is \texttt{degree}, and \texttt{all} which contains it, on
\texttt{node\_degree} --- the one task where a primer states the requested
quantity verbatim. It is worth $+7.5$ $[+3.0, +12.0]$ and $+10.2$ $[+5.2,
+15.2]$ points to \texttt{qwen3-1.7b}, and $+11.7$ and $+11.6$ to
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think} (Table~\ref{tab:main-think}). To
\texttt{qwen3-4b} it is worth $-6.5$ $[-9.2, -4.0]$ and $-9.2$ $[-12.2,
-6.2]$, with non-overlapping intervals between the sizes. A primer that \emph{contains the answer} makes the larger model
worse; $26$ of its $29$ \texttt{degree} losses are off by one to three,
consistent with the primer competing with, rather than replacing, the
model's own enumeration of the encoding. Table~\ref{tab:density} shows
this is a ceiling artifact: \texttt{qwen3-4b} is at $99\%$ under
\texttt{none} at every density, leaving a primer no headroom and only the
cost of added tokens to register, while \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} falls from
$0.910$ to $0.300$ over the same range and does admit a positive effect.

\paragraph{Answer retrieval is partial even where it is complete in
principle.} The shortcut solver attains $1.00$ on \texttt{node\_degree}
under \texttt{degree} and \texttt{all}, and on \texttt{edge\_count} under
the same two, since summing a stated degree sequence yields $2m$. No arm
approaches that ceiling: the largest \texttt{edge\_count} gain anywhere is
\texttt{qwen3-4b} under \texttt{degree}, $+27.7$ points, still leaving
roughly $70\%$ of instances wrong against a bar of $100\%$. A primer that
fully determines the answer is not a test of graph reasoning, but neither
is it automatically exploited: it measures whether the model uses a fact it
was handed, and largely it does not. The ceilings were fit on GraphQA's
native $5$--$19$ node graphs; at $n{=}40$ the constant answers ``$40$'' and
``yes'' score $100\%$ on \texttt{node\_count} and \texttt{cycle\_check}, so
only the exact-theorem ceilings above transfer unchanged.

\paragraph{Trivially-answerable tasks respond to text, not structure.} The
largest effects in Table~\ref{tab:main-results} fall on
\texttt{node\_count}, whose gold answer is the constant $40$. At 1.7B every
condition improves on \texttt{none} ($31.5\%$), by $+2.0$ to $+67.2$
points, in an order that follows no property of content:
\texttt{clustering} $+67.2$, \texttt{all} $+47.0$, \texttt{filler} $+39.8$,
\texttt{components} $+21.2$, \texttt{degree} $+6.8$, \texttt{rwse} $+2.0$
(n.s.). At 4B the six are statistically indistinguishable, all $+8.0$ to
$+9.8$. In both arms the content-free \texttt{filler} is among the
strongest. The dominant \texttt{none} failure is the answer ``$39$'', an
off-by-one from the encoder's ``nodes $0, \dots, 39$'' preamble that any
interposed text appears to disrupt. We accordingly retract the reading of
this cell as a structural primer effect.

\paragraph{Prompt length is not a neutral covariate.}
\label{sec:results-length}

The \texttt{filler} condition is not inert. It significantly harms
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b} on \texttt{edge\_existence} ($-15.8$ $[-20.0, -11.8]$)
and \texttt{connected\_nodes} ($-6.2$ $[-10.2, -2.2]$), and
\texttt{qwen3-4b} on \texttt{connected\_nodes} ($-12.5$ $[-17.0, -8.0]$)
and \texttt{edge\_existence} ($-7.0$ $[-10.8, -3.5]$),
while significantly \emph{helping} both on the trivially-answerable
\texttt{node\_count}. At $n{=}40$ it adds $1{,}831$ characters, between
\texttt{clustering} ($1{,}631$) and \texttt{rwse} ($2{,}951$;
Table~\ref{tab:lengths}). Carrying no structural information by
construction, any effect it registers is attributable to length and
position alone \citep{liu2024lostinthemiddle,kamradt2023needle}, so
comparable effects measured against \texttt{none} cannot be credited to
content without it. Over a wider density range ($p$ up to $0.85$,
$2{,}774$ pairs, \texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think}) the filler costs $-6.3$ points
pooled ($p < 10^{-4}$): nothing significant at $p \le 0.35$, then $-9.5$,
$-9.3$, $-14.1$ and $-11.7$ at the four densest levels. Length costs more
on dense graphs than most primer contents gain anywhere here.

\paragraph{Chain-of-thought dominates every primer effect.}
\label{sec:results-cot}

Varying only the decoding mode, with checkpoint and prompts fixed
(Table~\ref{tab:cot}), is worth $+29.2$ points pooled and $+50.0$ at $p =
0.85$ --- an order of magnitude above the largest non-retrieval primer
effect measured, and consistent with \citet{wei2022chainofthought}. The
consequence is that the thinking arms sit at ceiling on
\texttt{cycle\_check}, \texttt{edge\_existence} and \texttt{node\_count}
under every condition, and \texttt{qwen3-4b-think} on five of six tasks,
leaving no headroom for a primer effect. Primers do change how often those
arms fail to terminate --- a process effect, not an accuracy one. For
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think}, \texttt{degree} truncates $3.4$ points more
often than \texttt{none} ($p < 0.001$), while \texttt{all},
\texttt{filler} and \texttt{clustering} truncate $1.3$--$3.4$ points less.
For \texttt{qwen3-4b-think} every primer truncates $2.0$ to $4.3$ points
less (all $p < 0.001$).

\paragraph{The one cell that survives every control.}
\label{sec:results-headline}

One cell clears every control, at four times the power of any cell in
Table~\ref{tab:main-results}. On a dedicated sweep of $400$ graphs per
density level, \texttt{clustering} improves \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} on
\texttt{node\_degree} by $+3.8$ points $[+1.5, +6.2]$, and an independent
sample reproduces it at $+4.3$ $[+1.9, +6.6]$ ($p = 0.0017$ and $0.0006$;
Table~\ref{tab:headline}). It is positive at every density level and
attributable neither to answer leakage (ceiling $0.08$) nor to length
(\texttt{clustering} is shorter than \texttt{filler}, which does not move
this task). The main sweep is consistent but underpowered here, measuring
$+3.0$ ($p = 0.256$) at $100$ graphs per cell. The effect does not survive
into the thinking arm ($+1.0$, $p = 0.694$), and \texttt{qwen3-4b} cannot
test it at all, being saturated under \texttt{none}: a replicated but
model- and mode-specific effect, not a general property of clustering
primers.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central negative result is a failure to replicate across scale: of the
$144$ (arm, task, primer) comparisons, no primer effect clearing both
\texttt{none} and \texttt{filler} in one model size does so in the other.
Three regularities account for most of the table, and none is the
hypothesized mechanism.

First, a primer helps reliably only where it states the requested quantity
verbatim, and then only partially: \texttt{degree} and \texttt{all} move
\texttt{node\_degree} and \texttt{edge\_count} and nothing else
consistently, while the statistics naming no answer survive both controls
in no more than one arm. This is answer retrieval done badly --- never more
than $28$ of $100$ available points, and at 4B backwards. Second, added text
is an active treatment regardless of content. It harms both sizes on
\texttt{edge\_existence} and \texttt{connected\_nodes} and helps both on
\texttt{node\_count}, at magnitudes ($-15.8$ to $+39.8$) exceeding nearly
every content effect here. A design comparing a primer only against
\texttt{none} therefore estimates content and length confounded, with the
length term often the larger. Third, the headroom is largely consumed by
the decoding mode. Chain-of-thought is worth $+29.2$ points pooled and
moves most cells to ceiling; where it does not saturate a task it censors
it, the $62$--$81\%$ truncation on \texttt{edge\_count} removing that task
from both thinking arms.

\looseness=-1
The interesting quantity is therefore not whether a structural preamble
helps, but where it \emph{could}: of the $24$ (arm, task) cells, $10$ sit
at or above $98\%$ under \texttt{none}, $2$ are censored, and $2$ more have
constant gold answers, and the few that remain are exactly where the
replicated \texttt{clustering} effect was found.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

This sweep covers only two model sizes (1.7B and 4B parameters) from a
single model family, so the findings may not generalize to substantially
larger models or to other architectures. Several (primer condition, task)
cells are known in advance to be shortcut-dominated at this graph size and
density range (Section~\ref{sec:setup}); results on those cells bound what
can be concluded about genuine graph reasoning even though we report them.
Graphs are drawn from a single generator family (Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi).
Whether primer effects transfer to other generators remains untested, and
\citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph} show the generator matters for encoding
effects. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:related-work}, varying the generator
family is not itself a safe way to test this: it confounds structure with
task difficulty.
Compute and time constraints limited this sweep to a single
node-count/density design rather than a broader sweep over graph sizes.

Finally, the length control is a bracket rather than an exact match: the
\texttt{filler} condition is longer than \texttt{degree} and
\texttt{clustering} and shorter than \texttt{rwse} and \texttt{all}
(Table~\ref{tab:lengths}), so it bounds the length term without
eliminating it. A design holding rendered length exactly constant while
varying structure would isolate content more sharply than subtracting a
bracketing control, and we regard that as the natural next step.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

This work evaluates open-weight language models on synthetically generated
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs. It involves no human subjects, no personal or
identifying data, and no annotation labour: gold answers are computed
programmatically from the graphs rather than collected from people. We see
no direct risk of harm arising from the findings, which are a negative
result about a prompting technique. The principal societal cost is the
compute expended, reported in Section~\ref{sec:setup}.
