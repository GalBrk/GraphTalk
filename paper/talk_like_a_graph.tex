\begin{abstract}
Prepending a short \emph{primer} of factual sentences about each node's
local structure --- degree, clustering coefficient, a random-walk
structural encoding, or connected-component membership --- before a
graph's standard text encoding is an intuitively appealing way to ease
graph-reasoning prompts for a large language model. Any apparent gain is
illusory, however, if a solver can recover the answer directly from the
primer text without ever consulting the graph, and it is confounded if the
primer merely lengthens the prompt. We introduce a graph-blind shortcut
solver as a content-only lower bound and a length-matched filler control as
a length-only upper bound. Evaluated at $n=40$ on \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} and
\texttt{qwen3-4b}, with and without chain-of-thought, we find that no primer
effect replicates across model scale. Across four arms, six tasks and six
primers, several cells beat both \texttt{none} and \texttt{filler}
significantly in one model size, but none does so in both. Nor is the filler
inert: it costs the thinking arm up to $14.1$ points on dense graphs and
harms both sizes on \texttt{edge\_existence}, and the largest apparent gains
fall on tasks whose answer is effectively constant, where content-free text
moves accuracy as much as a structural primer. Chain-of-thought on an
identical checkpoint yields a $+29.2$-point pooled improvement, an order of
magnitude larger than any primer effect measured. The effects that do appear
are organised by one variable: a primer's benefit falls as the model's own
accuracy without it rises, so that a primer stating the answer verbatim is
worth $+27.8$ points where the baseline is $0.02$ and $-6.5$ where it is
$0.99$ --- within a single model. Our graph-blind solver separates this from
a ceiling artifact: over $177$ cells that offer the model a substitute route
to the answer the effect tracks baseline at $r = -0.390$, while over $177$
baseline-matched cells that do not it tracks nothing ($r = +0.041$). The
relation replicates on eleven held-out arms spanning $0.6$--$14$B and two
model families, where parameter count adds nothing once baseline is included
($p = 0.64$). One cell survives every
control: \texttt{clustering} on \texttt{node\_degree} for
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b}, $+3.8$ points, replicated at $+4.3$ ($p=0.0006$).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph} demonstrate that a large language model's
accuracy on graph-reasoning questions depends substantially on how the
graph is encoded as text, independent of its underlying reasoning ability.
Their study established foundational baselines by varying the encoding function 
(e.g., adjacency lists, incident lists) while holding the question and graph fixed.

This work poses a complementary question: rather than altering how edges
are described, does prepending a short \emph{primer} of derived,
node-level facts before the graph encoding help a model answer questions
about that graph? While we adopt the GraphQA benchmark to evaluate these tasks, 
our intervention—the primer—introduces an architectural variable absent from 
the original suite of encoding experiments. A primer lies outside the core encoding 
vocabulary: it is additional, redundant information computed from the graph and 
expressed in plain sentences. 

Two confounds make such an effect hard to read. If a primer helps because the model reads
the answer out of the preamble rather than reasoning over the graph, that is
answer leakage, not reasoning. Furthermore, if it helps or hurts because it
lengthens the prompt, that says nothing about its \emph{content}:
\citet{liu2024lostinthemiddle} and the needle-in-a-haystack protocol of
\citet{kamradt2023needle} demonstrate that context position and length alone move
retrieval accuracy.

This paper makes two contributions to isolate these confounds. First, a single, deterministic primer
renderer producing seven conditions (Section~\ref{sec:primers}) that differ
only in content, allowing differences between them to be interpreted as genuine content effects --- among them a length-matched, information-free \texttt{filler} condition bounding the length term.
Second, a \emph{shortcut solver} (Section~\ref{sec:shortcuts}) that lower-bounds what a primer leaks by answering each GraphQA task using only the preamble text. We evaluate both in a
density sweep on \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} and \texttt{qwen3-4b}
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

We adopt the GraphQA benchmark and task definitions introduced by \citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph}. While their foundational work established that encoding choices (and underlying graph generators) materially affect LLM accuracy, our study shifts the focus to the impact of explicit structural preambles. To avoid confounding structural topology with task difficulty, we explicitly fix graph size and edge density (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) rather than sampling structure incidentally.

A broader literature probes LLM graph reasoning capabilities.
\citet{wang2023nlgraph} report that LLMs are brittle to spurious
correlations in graph and problem structure; \citet{guo2023gpt4graph} find
that models struggle to exploit standard graph description languages directly; and
\citet{jin2024largelanguagemodelsongraphs} survey the wider space of
LLM-graph integration. \citet{dziri2023faithandfate} argue that
transformers solve compositional tasks by linearized subgraph matching
rather than systematic multi-step reasoning, raising the question a
primer's apparent benefit also raises: is a gain evidence of reasoning, or
of a shorter pattern to match against? \citet{geirhos2020shortcut} frame
this generally as \emph{shortcut learning} --- a rule that performs well on
a benchmark without solving the intended problem. Our shortcut solver
(Section~\ref{sec:shortcuts}) operationalizes that concern specifically for the primer
channel.

The RWSE condition adapts the random-walk structural encoding of
\citet{dwivedi2022graph}, developed for graph transformers; we render its
return probabilities as sentences rather than feeding them to an attention
mechanism. The chain-of-thought arm follows \citet{wei2022chainofthought},
and our paired testing follows \citet{dror2018hitchhikers}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Primers}
\label{sec:primers}

A primer is a short preamble of factual sentences describing a graph's
local or global structure, prepended to the standard text encoding and the
task question. A single renderer produces every condition, ensuring all
node-level facts share identical sentence framing (``Node $N$ has\dots'')
and formatting. We evaluate seven conditions. One is a baseline (\textbf{none}: the encoding with no primer). Five inject structural facts: \textbf{components}, one graph-level sentence giving the number of connected components; \textbf{degree}, each node's exact degree; \textbf{clustering}, each node's clustering coefficient; \textbf{rwse}, each node's random-walk return probabilities
\citep{dwivedi2022graph}; and \textbf{all}, the latter three combined. The
seventh, \textbf{filler}, is a content-free control of comparable length
(``Node $N$ is simply present within the graph $G$.''). It isolates content
effects from the confound of merely lengthening the prompt.

\subsection{Shortcut solvers}
\label{sec:shortcuts}

For a primer's measured accuracy to constitute evidence of graph
reasoning, it must exceed what is recoverable from the primer text alone.
We construct a shortcut solver that combines sixteen exact rules, one heuristic rule, eight fitted rules (whose fitting and scoring graph sets are kept structurally disjoint), and an exact enumeration bound for small graphs. Being structurally incapable of observing the actual graph object, its accuracy on a (primer, task) cell provides a model-independent bound. Results must be read against this \emph{shortcut ceiling} rather than against zero. Where a primer fully determines the answer --- a ceiling of $1.0$ --- model accuracy on that cell is uninformative about graph reasoning, however high (Table~\ref{tab:shortcut}).

\subsection{Prompt assembly \& Significance testing}
\label{sec:prompts}

Each prompt concatenates the primer, the graph's text encoding, and the
task question. Prompts are rebuilt from a canonicalized, parsed
representation of each graph rather than reused verbatim from the source
dataset, ensuring reproducible node and edge ordering. 

Every comparison reported here is paired: a primer condition is compared
against \texttt{none} on the same graph, with the same question and gold
answer. Pairs in which either arm reached the output-token cap are dropped
from that comparison rather than scored as incorrect. The primary test is the exact McNemar test \citep{mcnemar1947note},
Benjamini--Hochberg corrected across the six primer conditions within a
task \citep{benjaminihochberg1995controlling} --- $q=0.05$. For interpreted comparisons, we additionally run paired permutation tests and a paired bootstrap $95\%$ confidence interval on the effect size.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks.} We evaluate on six GraphQA tasks from
\citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph}: node count, edge count, node degree,
connected nodes, edge existence, and cycle check.

\paragraph{Models.} We compare \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} and \texttt{qwen3-4b}
from the Qwen3 model family \citep{qwen2025qwen3}, each evaluated in a
non-thinking (greedy decoding, no chain-of-thought) and a thinking
(chain-of-thought-enabled; \citealp{wei2022chainofthought}) variant, for
four model arms in total. We selected the Qwen3 family for its strong architectural priors in processing structured data and code. Because Qwen3 utilizes Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) and is optimized for long-context syntactic tracking, it possesses robust native mechanisms for spatial enumeration, such as traversing an incident list. Evaluating both a base and a reasoning-enabled checkpoint allows us to observe how explicitly injected structural priors (the primers) interact with the model's internal computational budget and its inherent topological representation.

\paragraph{Sweep design.} All four arms are evaluated on one shared set of
prompts. Graphs are Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi \citep{erdosrenyi1959random} with a 
fixed node count of 40 and edge density swept over four levels 
($p \in \{0.10, 0.20, 0.35, 0.50\}$). Every combination of the seven primer 
conditions and six tasks is evaluated on 100 graphs per (density, task) cell, 
yielding 16{,}800 prompts per model arm. Generation is greedy, with a raised output 
budget (up to 8192 tokens). Auxiliary sweeps include a fixed \texttt{node\_degree} 
sweep at $400$ graphs per density level, and an extended range sweep up to $p=0.85$.

\paragraph{Held-out arms.} Eleven further model arms are held out as a
replication set. No checkpoint among them appears in the main sweep, so
neither the model nor the corpus was used to develop any claim here. Eight
are evaluated on the published GraphQA split (30 graphs per cell, five
tasks, six conditions): \texttt{qwen3-8b} and \texttt{qwen3-14b}, and
\texttt{gemma-4-E4B-it} and \texttt{gemma-4-12B-it} from a second model
family, each with and without chain-of-thought. Three more ---
\texttt{qwen3-0.6b} with and without chain-of-thought, and
\texttt{qwen35-2b} --- answer a 100-graph \texttt{\{none, degree\}} probe
over all six tasks. Together they extend the comparison from $1.7$--$4$B in
one family to $0.6$--$14$B in two.

\paragraph{Metrics and reproducibility.} We use exact match for integer-
and boolean-valued tasks and set-F1 for connected nodes, reporting every
result against both \texttt{none} and the corresponding shortcut ceiling.
Code, prompt sets, and raw generations are released with the paper.

\paragraph{Known validity caveats.} Several cells are shortcut-dominated at
this graph size rather than genuine tests of graph reasoning: any primer
with one sentence per node lets node count be recovered by counting
sentences; cycle check's gold answer is ``yes'' for nearly every graph past
the sparsest level; and \texttt{degree} states the node-degree answer
verbatim. The one cell established as free of this contamination is node
degree under \texttt{\{none, components, clustering, filler\}}.

Reading the reversal above as an effect of model size is a coincidence of
which tasks we chose. A variable that has nothing to do with parameter count
explains it better: how accurate the model already was on that cell without
any primer. The rest of this section tests that claim three ways --- against
an instrument we already have, against an experimental manipulation, and on
models we did not use to formulate it.

\paragraph{The shortcut solver separates two kinds of primer.} The
graph-blind solver of Section~\ref{sec:shortcuts} measures, per (task,
primer), how much of the answer is recoverable from the primer text alone.
That measurement is an instrument, not just a control: it splits the
conditions into those that offer the model a substitute route to the answer
and those that add text without one. Table~\ref{tab:baseline} applies the
split to the $354$ cells of the four non-degenerate tasks. Where a
substitute route exists, the effect declines with baseline at $r = -0.390$
($p = 8 \cdot 10^{-8}$) and crosses zero at $0.79$. Where none exists, there
is no relation at all: $r = +0.041$ ($p = 0.59$), and for the content-free
\texttt{filler} alone, $r = -0.006$ ($p = 0.97$).

This is the control that regression to the mean has to survive, and does
not. Both groups hold $177$ cells, drawn from the same four tasks, with
matched baseline distributions; a cell at $0.99$ has the same nowhere-to-go
pressure in either. Only the primers that hand the model an answer show the
slope.

\paragraph{The same pattern appears when difficulty is manipulated, not
observed.} Table~\ref{tab:continuum} holds the arm, the task and the primer
fixed and varies only edge density, which drives \texttt{node\_degree}
accuracy from $0.95$ down to $0.44$. The \texttt{degree} primer's benefit
rises monotonically across the seven levels, from $+3.1$ to $+34.2$ points,
$r = -0.975$ ($p = 0.0002$). No sampling artifact produces a monotone ramp
of that size against an experimentally set variable.

The same table shows the second effect, running the other way. The
content-free \texttt{filler} is harmless on sparse graphs and costs $-14.1$
points on dense ones, $r = +0.953$ ($p = 0.0009$); the plain arm agrees
($r = +0.805$, $p = 0.029$). Added text is not a fixed tax. Its cost grows
as the task gets harder, exactly where an answer-stating primer's benefit
grows. The two mechanisms are separable and have opposite signs, which is
why pooling all conditions into one correlation understates both.

\paragraph{The relation holds on eleven unseen arms and a second model
family.} On the held-out arms of Section~\ref{sec:setup} --- $0.6$B to
$14$B, Qwen3 and Gemma --- the relation reappears at full strength where a
substitute route exists: $r = -0.662$ over $73$ cells
($p = 2 \cdot 10^{-10}$). Over the $54$ cells without one it is not
significant, $r = -0.206$ ($p = 0.14$), so the contrast survives, though
less sharply than in Table~\ref{tab:baseline}; these two groups are not
baseline-matched as those were ($0.903 \pm 0.206$ against
$0.949 \pm 0.151$), which is why Table~\ref{tab:baseline} and not this
comparison carries the control. Regressing the effect on baseline, $\log$
parameter count and reasoning mode together gives a baseline coefficient of
$-30.7$ ($p = 1 \cdot 10^{-10}$) and a parameter coefficient of $+0.39$
($p = 0.64$). Once baseline is in the model, scale contributes nothing
measurable. Gemma alone cannot confirm the relation --- its baselines have
almost no spread left ($0.982 \pm 0.028$, $r = -0.280$, $p = 0.14$) --- but
it does not contradict it either.

One quantity does not transfer: the crossover. It sits at $0.79$ on the
$n{=}40$ corpus and near $0.99$ on the published split, so the threshold is
a property of a corpus rather than a constant, and predicting the sign of a
small effect on a new corpus from our fit does no better than chance.

\paragraph{Route substitution.} A primer that states the answer offers a
second way to produce it, and the model takes that route. Whether this helps
depends on whether the offered route is more reliable than the one the model
already had, which is what the baseline measures. Below the crossover,
copying beats computing; above it, copying is the noisier option and the
primer is a net cost. Within \texttt{qwen3-4b} alone the \texttt{degree}
primer is worth $+27.8$ points on \texttt{edge\_count}, where that arm's
baseline is $0.02$, and $-6.5$ on \texttt{node\_degree}, where it is $0.99$,
with no between-model comparison involved. This subsumes the non-replication
result: an effect measured at one baseline need not transfer to a model at a
different one.

The practical consequence is that a primer effect reported against a
no-primer baseline, without reporting that baseline, is uninterpretable. The
same intervention reads as $+27.8$ or $-6.5$ depending on a number the table
does not otherwise print.

\paragraph{Answer retrieval is partial even where it is complete in
principle.} The shortcut solver attains $1.00$ on \texttt{node\_degree}
under \texttt{degree} and \texttt{all}, and on \texttt{edge\_count} under
the same two. No arm approaches that ceiling: the largest \texttt{edge\_count} gain anywhere is
\texttt{qwen3-4b} under \texttt{degree}, $+27.7$ points, still leaving
roughly $70\%$ of instances wrong against a bar of $100\%$. 

\paragraph{Trivially-answerable tasks respond to text, not structure.} The
largest apparent effects in Table~\ref{tab:main-results} fall on \texttt{node\_count}. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:setup}, its gold answer is the constant $40$ at this graph size. In both arms, the content-free \texttt{filler} is among the strongest conditions. The dominant \texttt{none} failure is the answer ``$39$'', an off-by-one from the encoder's ``nodes $0, \dots, 39$'' preamble that any interposed text appears to disrupt. We retract the reading of this cell as a structural primer effect.

\paragraph{Prompt length is not a neutral covariate.}
The \texttt{filler} condition is not inert. It significantly harms
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b} on \texttt{edge\_existence} ($-15.8$ $[-20.0, -11.8]$)
and \texttt{connected\_nodes} ($-6.2$ $[-10.2, -2.2]$), and
\texttt{qwen3-4b} on \texttt{connected\_nodes} ($-12.5$ $[-17.0, -8.0]$)
and \texttt{edge\_existence} ($-7.0$ $[-10.8, -3.5]$). Over a wider density range ($p$ up to $0.85$, $2{,}774$ pairs, \texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think}), the filler costs $-6.3$ points pooled ($p < 10^{-4}$): nothing significant at $p \le 0.35$, followed by sharp declines of $-9.5$, $-9.3$, $-14.1$, and $-11.7$ at the four densest levels. Length incurs a heavier penalty on dense graphs than most primer contents gain anywhere here, confirming that comparable effects measured against \texttt{none} cannot be credited to content without a length control.

\paragraph{Chain-of-thought dominates every primer effect.}
Activating chain-of-thought reasoning, while fixing the checkpoint and prompts, yields an average improvement of $+29.2$ points (and $+50.0$ at $p = 0.85$) --- an order of magnitude above the largest non-retrieval primer effect measured. The consequence is that the thinking arms sit at ceiling on \texttt{cycle\_check}, \texttt{edge\_existence} and \texttt{node\_count} under every condition, leaving no headroom for an observable primer effect. 

\paragraph{The one cell that survives every control.}
On a dedicated sweep of $400$ graphs per density level, \texttt{clustering} improves \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} on
\texttt{node\_degree} by $+3.8$ points $[+1.5, +6.2]$, and an independent
sample reproduces it at $+4.3$ $[+1.9, +6.6]$ ($p = 0.0017$ and $0.0006$;
Table~\ref{tab:headline}). It is positive at every density level and
attributable neither to answer leakage nor to length. However, the effect does not survive into the thinking arm, and \texttt{qwen3-4b} cannot test it at all, being saturated under \texttt{none}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central negative result of this study is a failure to replicate across
scale: no primer effect that clears both the \texttt{none} baseline and the 
\texttt{filler} control in one model size does so in the other. Three regularities 
account for most of the variations observed, and none align with the mechanism a 
primer was hypothesized to supply.

First, a primer helps reliably only where it states the requested quantity
verbatim, and its sign is set by the model's baseline accuracy rather than
by its size (Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}). The natural reading is
route substitution: the primer supplies a second way to produce the answer,
the model takes it, and this is a gain exactly when the offered route is
more reliable than the one the model already had. At 1.7B, whose own
enumeration of a dense incident list is unreliable, the text shortcut is an
improvement; at 4B, whose enumeration is already correct on $99\%$ of
instances, it is a substitution of a noisier route for a better one.

We emphasise that this is a claim about behaviour, not about circuits. We
measured no attention weights, activations or internal states, and the data
place at least one limit on any account framed as the larger model
\emph{rejecting} the primer: \texttt{qwen3-4b-think} answers correctly on
all $29$ of the instances the \texttt{degree} primer breaks for
\texttt{qwen3-4b}, under that identical primer and identical weights. What
the primer disrupts is therefore recoverable within the same network once a
reasoning trace is permitted, which is not what an interference account
would predict.

Second, added text acts as an active treatment regardless of its content. The
length-matched \texttt{filler} significantly harms both model sizes on
\texttt{edge\_existence} and \texttt{connected\_nodes} and significantly
helps both on \texttt{node\_count}, at magnitudes exceeding nearly every content 
effect measured here. An experimental design that compares a primer only against 
\texttt{none} estimates content and length confounded, with the length term frequently emerging as the larger of the two.

Third, the headroom in which any of this could be measured is largely
consumed by the decoding mode. Chain-of-thought moves most (task, arm) cells
to ceiling, and where it does not saturate a task it censors it instead. It
also changes how long the model generates before answering --- a measurable
effect on the process rather than on accuracy. For
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think}, \texttt{degree} raises the non-termination rate
by $3.4$ points over \texttt{none} ($p < 0.001$) while the content-free
\texttt{filler} lowers it, and outside \texttt{edge\_count} that primer
produces $137$ non-terminating generations of which only $7$ also fail to
terminate under \texttt{none}. These are new failures attributable to the
primer, not to the instance. We report the effect at that level and do not
infer a termination mechanism from it: response length is an observable,
the policy that produced it is not.

\section{Future Work: Mechanistic Interpretability}
\label{sec:future-work}

The behavioural regularities reported here---a primer effect whose sign
tracks baseline accuracy, and a condition-dependent shift in how long the
thinking arms generate---are consistent with several internal accounts and
identify none of them. That is a mandate for mechanistic interpretability. We plan to apply activation patching and causal abstraction to Qwen3's
grouped-query attention heads during these tasks, intervening on the heads
that read the incident list while a primer is present, to test whether the
substitution we observe behaviourally corresponds to an identifiable
change in how the answer is computed.

Two cheaper experiments come first, because they discriminate between
behavioural accounts without any interpretability machinery, and both are
prompt-only changes against the existing corpus. A \emph{single-fact} primer
stating only the queried node's degree keeps the answer available while
removing the other $39$ sentences, separating interference from route
substitution. A \emph{corrupted} primer stating a wrong degree measures
reliance directly: a model that copies collapses, a model that computes is
unaffected, and the resulting reliance curve is the quantitative form of the
prediction in Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}. Extending the same prompt
file down and up the Qwen3 ladder (0.6B, 8B, 14B) would turn the two-point
sign reversal into a curve, and because 0.6B to 1.7B varies width at
constant depth while 1.7B to 4B adds depth, it would also separate those two
axes. Whether the pattern holds outside one model family remains open.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We evaluated seven primer conditions across six GraphQA tasks, four
densities, and four model arms at $n{=}40$, totaling $67{,}200$
generations. Two controls rigorously bound the result: a graph-blind solver measuring 
answer retrieval, and a length-matched, content-free primer measuring prompt length. 
No primer effect clears both controls across both model sizes. One exception survives 
every control and an independent replication: \texttt{clustering} on \texttt{node\_degree} 
for \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} ($+3.8$ and $+4.3$ points). We interpret the broader result not as 
evidence that structural preambles cannot help, but that most (model, task) pairs at this
scale afford no interpretable test of whether they do. The effects that do
appear are organised by baseline accuracy rather than by scale
(Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}), a dissociation that our graph-blind
solver isolates and that thirteen held-out arms across two model families
reproduce: a primer stating the answer is worth
$+27.8$ points where the model's own accuracy is $0.02$ and $-6.5$ where it
is $0.99$, within a single model, with the sign crossing near $0.8$. The
practical reading is that prepending a local fact substitutes one route to
the answer for another, and is a gain only while the model's own route is
the worse of the two.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The main sweep covers only two model sizes (1.7B and 4B parameters) from a
single model family; the held-out arms extend the range to $0.6$--$14$B and
a second family, but on smaller corpora and fewer conditions, so the
findings may not generalize to substantially larger models or to other
architectures. The baseline relation of
Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline} is measured against each arm's own
accuracy, and substituting an independent difficulty estimate --- the mean
\texttt{none} accuracy of the other three arms on the same cell --- leaves
no significant relation ($r = +0.113$, $p = 0.13$). We therefore read the
effect as governed by a model's own competence on an item set, not by any
difficulty intrinsic to the items. Several cells are known in advance to be
shortcut-dominated at this graph size and density range; results on those cells bound what
can be concluded about genuine graph reasoning even though we report them.
Graphs are drawn from a single generator family (Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi).
Whether primer effects transfer to other generators remains untested.
Finally, the length control is a bracket rather than an exact match: the
\texttt{filler} condition is longer than \texttt{degree} and
\texttt{clustering} and shorter than \texttt{rwse} and \texttt{all}, so it bounds the length term without eliminating it. A design holding rendered length exactly constant while
varying structure would isolate content more sharply, and we regard that as the natural next step.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

This work evaluates open-weight language models on synthetically generated
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs. It involves no human subjects, no personal or
identifying data, and no annotation labour. We see no direct risk of harm arising 
from the findings. The principal societal cost is the compute expended.
