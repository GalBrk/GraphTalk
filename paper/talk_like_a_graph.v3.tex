\begin{abstract}
Prepending a \emph{primer} of factual sentences about a graph's structure ---
each node's degree, clustering coefficient, random-walk return probability or
component --- to its text encoding is a natural way to help a language model
answer questions about that graph. Across $84{,}000$ generations from four
Qwen3 arms (1.7B and 4B, thinking off and on) on six GraphQA tasks and seven
edge densities over $40$-node Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs, we separate two
things a primer can do: determine the answer by itself, which a graph-blind
solver detects, and add text, whose cost we fit across five conditions
spanning $37$ to $4{,}691$ characters. Length turns out to be close to free
(a length term of $-0.3$ points per thousand characters, positive in $8$ of
$16$ cells). Content that determines the answer is not: it is worth about
$+16$ points where the model is failing and nothing where it is not, and on
\texttt{node\_degree} it costs \texttt{qwen3-4b} $12$ points at $p{=}.50$
while gaining it $27$ at $p{=}.85$ --- same arm, same task, same primer, the
sign following the accuracy the cell already had. The generated text shows
why: the model stops counting and starts looking up, its median response
falling from $273$ tokens to $34$. Structural side information that does not
determine the answer is worth $+2$ to $+4$ points on average in the same
window --- by primer, from $-0.3$ to $+5.3$ --- and nothing outside it, and more than half the cells of a standard sweep sit above
$0.90$ accuracy, where neither can show anything.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph} show that a language model's accuracy on
graph-reasoning questions varies substantially with the text encoding of the
graph. We ask whether prepending a \emph{primer} --- a short preamble of
derived, node-level facts such as each node's degree or clustering
coefficient --- improves the answers, and, when it changes them, what it
changes.

Two things have to be separated before that question has an answer. A primer
may help because the model reads the answer off it rather than reasoning over
the graph, which is the shortcut-learning concern of
\citet{geirhos2020shortcut} and the alternative to reasoning that
\citet{dziri2023faithandfate} describe. And a primer may move accuracy simply
by being there: added context is reported to degrade reasoning on its own
\citep{shi2023distracted,levy2024sametask}. We address the first with a
graph-blind solver that reads the primer text and never the graph, which says
for each condition whether the primer determines the answer by itself; and the
second by fitting the effect on primer length across five conditions spanning
$37$ to $4{,}691$ characters, rather than trusting a single matched control.

Both separations change the conclusion. Length is close to free: fitted within
each (arm, task), the length term averages $-0.3$ points per thousand
characters and is positive in $8$ of $16$ cells. What is not free is content
that determines the answer --- and the surprise is that it frequently makes
the answers worse. On \texttt{node\_degree} the \texttt{degree} primer prints
the requested number verbatim, and \texttt{qwen3-4b} falls from $99$ to $87$
points at $p{=}.50$, breaking $13$ items for each one it fixes. The generated
text shows the mechanism: the model stops counting and starts looking up, its
median response falling from $273$ tokens to $34$.

The same primer, the same task and the same arm gain $+27$ points at
$p{=}.85$, where that arm scores $33$ without it. What decides the sign is
neither the primer nor the task nor the model, but the accuracy the cell
already had. Prompt-level graph interventions have been proposed before ---
\citet{wang2023nlgraph} give \emph{Build-a-Graph} and \emph{Algorithmic}
prompting, \citet{perozzi2024letyourgraph} learn a continuous encoding --- and
what we add is a controlled account of when one helps, when it hurts, and how
much of the help is leakage, read from $84{,}000$ generations on $40$-node
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs \citep{erdosrenyi1959random}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Primers and controls}
\label{sec:primers}

A prompt is a primer, then the graph in the \texttt{incident} encoding, then
the question. Seven conditions share one renderer, so they differ in content
and never in format: \texttt{none}; four content primers reporting node
degree, clustering coefficient, random-walk return probability
\citep{dwivedi2022graph} and connected-component membership; \texttt{all},
which renders degree, clustering and \texttt{rwse} together; and
\texttt{filler}. Mean primer lengths are $37$, $891$, $1{,}629$, $2{,}949$ and
$4{,}691$ characters for \texttt{components}, \texttt{degree},
\texttt{clustering}, \texttt{rwse} and \texttt{all}, and $1{,}829$ for
\texttt{filler}.

\texttt{filler} is a \emph{degenerate-content} preamble of comparable length
to \texttt{clustering}: forty sentences of the form ``Node $i$ is simply
present within the graph $G$''. It is not content-free --- those sentences
state the vertex set exactly, and a solver reading only \texttt{filler} scores
$1.00$ on \texttt{node\_count} --- and it is length-matched to
\texttt{clustering} alone, so we use it as one condition among several rather
than as the reference a content effect is defined against.

The control that does the work is the graph-blind solver. It parses the
rendered primer text and applies exact rules, never touching the graph, so its
score per (task, condition) is what the primer alone determines. We call a
condition \emph{answer-carrying} on a task when that bar is at least $0.85$;
the bars are bimodal --- $18$ of the $42$ (task, condition) bars sit at
exactly $1.000$ and the highest of the rest is $0.746$ --- so any threshold
between them gives the same split (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:shortcut}).

\subsection{Corpus, arms and measurement}
\label{sec:setup}
% ci_table.tex cites the extraction and truncation discussion, which is here.
\label{sec:extraction}

Four arms answer one shared prompt set: Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B
\citep{qwen2025qwen3}, each with native thinking mode off (\texttt{qwen3-1.7b},
\texttt{qwen3-4b}) and on (\texttt{-think}). Graphs are Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi with
$n{=}40$ at $p\in\{.10,.20,.35,.50\}$, $100$ graphs per (task, density) cell,
six GraphQA tasks and seven conditions, extended on \texttt{node\_degree} and
\texttt{edge\_existence} to $p\in\{.65,.75,.85\}$. Decoding is greedy. The main
sweep ran with an $8{,}192$-token budget on every arm; the high-density
extension ran at $2{,}048$ on the plain arms and $8{,}192$ on the thinking
arms, so truncation rates are not comparable across those two bands and are
reported separately (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:hitcap}).

Three measurement decisions change what the numbers mean. \emph{Exact match}
is the outcome for every task; on \texttt{connected\_nodes} the usual set-F1
sits at $0.93$--$1.00$ in every cell while exact match spans $0.48$--$0.92$, so
F1 cannot separate the conditions. \emph{Balanced accuracy} accompanies
accuracy on \texttt{edge\_existence}, where the share of node pairs that are
edges rises from $10\%$ to $85\%$ across our density range: raw accuracy
recovers with density while balanced accuracy falls to chance
(Figure~\ref{fig:balanced}). And \emph{truncation is an outcome}, not a missing
value, so a pair is dropped when either side truncated --- the convention least
favourable to the primer --- and rates are reported alongside.
\texttt{node\_count} and \texttt{cycle\_check} have a constant gold answer at
$n{=}40$ and hence a majority-class baseline of $1.000$; they are reported in
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:pertask-nodecount} onward but excluded from every
summary over tasks. Differences are paired
on the shared graph, tested with exact McNemar \citep{mcnemar1947note} and
corrected with Benjamini--Hochberg within each (arm, task) family. Extraction
is not a confound: $99.84\%$ of the $84{,}000$ generations parse, and $99\%$
of the $133$ that do not are truncated, $128$ of them on
\texttt{cycle\_check}.

\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Most of a standard sweep cannot show an effect}
\label{sec:results-window}

\input{v3_window_table.tex}

A primer can only move a cell that is not already at floor or ceiling, and
most cells are. Of the $324$ side-information cells on the four informative
tasks, $172$ --- $53\%$ --- sit above $0.90$ accuracy without any primer and
cannot show a gain by construction. Table~\ref{tab:window} bins every cell by
that baseline and splits it by whether the primer determines the answer. Both
columns show the same shape: nothing at the top of the range, and whatever
movement exists concentrated between roughly $0.25$ and $0.90$; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:headroom} counts the same effects by significance.

The two columns differ in size, not in shape. Inside that window a primer
that carries the answer is worth about $+16$ points on average; genuine side
information is worth $+2$ to $+4$. Above $0.90$ both are indistinguishable
from zero ($-1.4$ and $-0.8$). Per primer, inside the window, only
\texttt{all} ($+5.3$), \texttt{clustering} ($+2.9$) and \texttt{rwse}
($+2.2$) are worth anything as side information; \texttt{components} averages
$-0.3$, and its one large effect turns out not to be content at all
(Section~\ref{sec:results-behaviour}).

\paragraph{Thinking mode closes the window.} The window is a property of the
arm as much as of the task. Turning thinking on raises the median baseline
from $56.5$ to $79.0$ on the 1.7B checkpoint and from $87.0$ to $97.0$ on the
4B one, which removes the cells a primer could have moved: of $110$ cells per
arm, $60$ lie in the $0.25$--$0.90$ window for \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} and only
$15$ for \texttt{qwen3-4b-think}. Within whatever window each arm has left, no
primer is best twice --- \texttt{all} is the best of the five on
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b} ($+9.8$) and the worst on both 4B arms ($-9.5$, $-13.0$);
\texttt{degree} leads on \texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think} ($+12.3$),
\texttt{clustering} on \texttt{qwen3-4b} ($+7.3$) and \texttt{components} on
\texttt{qwen3-4b-think} ($+27.7$). A primer selected on one arm does not
transfer to another (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}).

\paragraph{Leakage is real but far from complete.} Where the graph-blind
solver scores $1.00$ --- the primer determines the answer outright --- the
model still has to find it, and mostly does not. On \texttt{node\_degree} the
recovered fraction runs from $0.99$ on \texttt{qwen3-4b-think} down to $0.43$
on \texttt{qwen3-1.7b}; on \texttt{edge\_count}, where the answer requires
summing the stated degrees rather than reading one of them, it runs $0.30$ on
\texttt{qwen3-4b} and $0.006$ on \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} under \texttt{all}. A
primer that contains the answer is not the same as a model that gives it,
and the gap between the two is where the arms differ most.

\subsection{The same primer helps and harms the same model on the same task}
\label{sec:results-paradox}

The cleanest demonstration needs no comparison across arms. On
\texttt{node\_degree}, \texttt{qwen3-4b} and the \texttt{degree} primer, which
prints the queried node's degree verbatim, the effect tracks the baseline
across the seven densities: $-6, -1, -7$ and $-12$ at $p{=}.10$ to $.50$,
where the arm scores $99$--$100$ without the primer, then $-6$ at $p{=}.65$
(baseline $82$), $+7$ at $p{=}.75$ (baseline $50$) and $+27$ at $p{=}.85$
(baseline $33$). Same
arm, same task, same primer; the sign flips as the task gets hard enough for
the model to need it.

The losses are not the cost of the added text. At $p{=}.50$ the primer breaks
$13$ items and fixes $1$, and neither cell truncates at all. The response
length says what happened: the median falls from $273$ new tokens under
\texttt{none} to $34$ under \texttt{degree}, while \texttt{filler} --- longer
than \texttt{degree} --- leaves it at $266$ and costs $2$ points. The model
abandons a reliable count and reads a number off the primer instead, and the
lookup is worse than the count.

\subsection{What the primer changes in the response}
\label{sec:results-behaviour}

The substitution is visible directly, and it pays when what it displaces was
failing. On \texttt{edge\_count} at $p{=}.50$, $7\%$ of
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b}'s responses under \texttt{none} state the handshake
shortcut --- sum the degrees, divide by two --- and the rest enumerate edges.
Under \texttt{degree}, which lists all $40$ degrees, $100\%$ state it, and
truncation falls from $29\%$ to $20\%$. At $p{=}.10$ the switch runs $67\%$ to
$99\%$. That gain is density-robust rather than a pooled average hiding one
cell: $+22, +30, +24, +33$ across the four main densities.

The other robust gain we measure is not information transfer at all. The
\texttt{components} primer on \texttt{qwen3-4b}/\texttt{connected\_nodes} runs
$+7, +11, +10, +9$ from a $37$-character preamble --- but at $n{=}40$ every
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graph at $p\ge.20$ is connected, so at three of those four
densities the primer emits the same sentence about all $100$ graphs, and the
gain is larger there than at $p{=}.10$, where $52$ of $100$ graphs have more
than one component and the string actually varies. Whatever that preamble
buys, it is not graph-specific content. The same account covers the largest
primer effects in the sweep: on \texttt{node\_count}, whose gold answer is
always $40$, interposing almost any text between the encoder's ``nodes
$0,\dots,39$'' preamble and the question interrupts an off-by-one that
otherwise costs \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} $68.5\%$ of its answers.

The direction of the length change is task-specific, so neither ``primers make
responses longer'' nor its converse holds. On
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b}/\texttt{edge\_existence} the same primers make the model
write more ($206$ tokens under \texttt{none} against $352$ under
\texttt{all}); on \texttt{qwen3-4b}/\texttt{connected\_nodes} \texttt{filler}
alone cuts the median from $233$ to $94$ (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:behaviour}).

Beyond $p{=}.50$ on \texttt{edge\_existence}, \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} stops
reasoning altogether and answers ``yes'' to $98$--$99\%$ of pairs. Neither
prompt length nor task difficulty triggers this: a ladder that raises $n$ at
fixed mean degree makes the prompt longer and the task harder without
producing it, and on the same graphs the model keeps working on the tasks
whose answer is a number rather than a boolean. What the two-valued answer
format permits is a cheap guess that is right most of the time --- and raw
accuracy rewards it while balanced accuracy does not
(Figure~\ref{fig:balanced}; Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:capitulation}).

\subsection{Position and composition}
\label{sec:results-limits}

\paragraph{Position inside the primer.} The primer lists nodes in id order, so
a retrieval account predicts that accuracy falls as the queried node moves down
it. Several primers show steep gradients --- \texttt{all} on
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think} loses $24.3$ points $[-33.5, -15.2]$ from the first
line to the last --- and node identity is not the cause, since over $19{,}600$
items a node's id correlates $+0.019$ with its degree and $+0.020$ with its
clustering coefficient. But \texttt{none} carries no primer and slopes too, by
$+25.0$ points on \texttt{qwen3-4b}/\texttt{connected\_nodes}, and
\texttt{filler} has the same forty id-ordered lines as \texttt{degree}, so it
is a second retrieval condition rather than a control. Testing each gradient
against its own \texttt{none} control, only $6$ of $48$ are distinguishable.
Position within the prompt moves accuracy; we cannot attribute it to the
primer.

\paragraph{Bundling.} \texttt{all} renders three primers together, at $86\%$
of their separate length ($4{,}691$ characters against $5{,}469$ summed), so
it carries every feature value they do. It does not carry their effect.
Over all $59$ (arm, task, density) cells, with no screen and no ratio, the
bundle falls $5.3$ points short of the sum of its parts $[2.8, 8.4]$,
in $44$ of the $59$ (sign test $p{=}2\times10^{-4}$), and three of the four
tasks exclude zero on their own: \texttt{connected\_nodes} $+4.8$ $[2.2,
7.4]$, \texttt{edge\_existence} $+4.8$ $[1.1, 8.9]$ and \texttt{node\_degree}
$+3.6$ $[1.7, 5.6]$, with \texttt{edge\_count} $+9.6$ $[-2.7, 23.7]$ too
noisy to call. The shortfall is therefore a property of bundling and not of
one task.

Its size is not identified. Regressing the bundle on the part-sum through
the origin gives $0.47$ over all tasks, $0.56$ without \texttt{edge\_count}
and $0.68$ on \texttt{node\_degree} alone, so ``about half'' would be an
\texttt{edge\_count} artifact. The per-cell median ratio, $0.50$ $[0.29,
0.71]$ over the $41$ cells whose parts sum to at least $4$ points, agrees in
direction but cannot be read against $1$: those cells enter on a noisy
denominator, and selecting on a noisy quantity inflates it, so under exact
additivity that estimator already returns $0.66$--$0.72$ depending on where
the true effects are centred, against which the observed value gives
$p{=}0.017$ to $0.063$. We report the shortfall in points and draw no
coefficient from the ratio (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:additivity}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Structural primers move GraphQA accuracy mainly by supplying the answer, and
only where the model is not already producing it. Where a primer determines
the answer it is worth about $+16$ points inside the difficulty window and
nothing above it; genuine structural side information is worth $+2$ to $+4$
inside that window and nothing outside it. What the primer does, visible in
the generated text, is offer a second procedure that the model takes: on
\texttt{edge\_count} the stated degrees replace enumeration with arithmetic in
every response, and on \texttt{node\_degree} the stated answer replaces a
reliable count with an unreliable lookup, costing $12$ points on a cell where
the model was already right $99$ times in $100$.

Two measurements make this legible and neither is standard. Fitting the effect
on primer length across conditions shows that length costs nothing here, so a
single matched control cannot be what a content effect is defined against. And
a graph-blind solver, scored per task and condition, separates the gains that
are leakage from the gains that are not. Without both, the majority of a
standard sweep --- more than half of its cells sit above $0.90$ --- is
uninformative about either.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Two model sizes from one family, one encoding, one template, one primer
position and greedy decoding throughout. All graphs are Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi, in
which local clustering is nearly uniform, so the \texttt{clustering} primer
carries little node-specific information. Most cells hold $100$ graphs, which
bounds what any single comparison establishes; the claims above are counts and
profiles over cells for that reason. The generation budget differs between the
main sweep and the high-density extension, so truncation rates are not
comparable across them.

Three limits bear on the behavioural results specifically. The procedure a
response states is identified by a single surface pattern over free text
(\texttt{divided by 2}, \texttt{sum of the degrees}, and \texttt{handshak}
as a prefix for \emph{handshake} and \emph{handshaking});
it is not hand-labelled, and a model may use a procedure it does not
narrate, so those shares are the rate at which a response \emph{says} it
took the arithmetic route rather than the rate at which it did. Position within the primer is confounded with node identity, and
the conditions available as controls carry the same positional structure, which
is why we report that effect as unattributed. And the response-length collapse
is attributed to the answer format by eliminating prompt length and difficulty,
not by manipulating the format directly. Four conditions would settle the open
questions: a shuffled-value primer as a stricter placebo, a corrupted primer to
separate copying from computation, single-fact bundles to separate dilution
from selection, and a permuted-order primer to break the position/identity
confound.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

This work evaluates open-weight language models on synthetically generated
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs. It involves no human subjects, no personal or
identifying data and no annotation labour. We see no direct risk of harm from
the findings; the principal cost is the GPU compute for approximately
$217{,}000$ generations.
