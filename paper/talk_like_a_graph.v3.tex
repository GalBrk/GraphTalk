\begin{abstract}
Prepending a \emph{primer} of factual sentences about a graph's structure
(node degree, clustering coefficient, random-walk return probabilities or
component count) to its text encoding is a natural way to assist a
language model on graph-reasoning questions. We evaluate seven primer
conditions against four controls jointly --- a graph-blind shortcut solver
refit at the evaluated graph size, a length-matched content-free filler, a
minimal-length content control, and a degree-preserving rewiring --- across
four Qwen3 arms (1.7B and 4B, with and without thinking mode), six GraphQA
tasks and seven edge densities on $40$-node Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs.
A primer's effect reverses across task, density and model size: the same
\texttt{degree} primer is worth $+26.5$ points against the length control
on \texttt{edge\_count} and $-6.0$ on \texttt{node\_degree}, where it
states the requested number outright. What the primer changes is which
procedure the model runs. Given a list of degrees, the share of responses
that sum them and halve rises from $7\%$ to $100\%$; where the primer
states the answer, the median response collapses from $273$ to $34$ tokens
and accuracy falls from $99$ to $87$. Accuracy then depends on where in the
$40$-line primer the queried node sits, by up to $21.6$ points; and
bundling three primers into one recovers a median $0.44$ of what they are
worth separately, losing gains and damage alike. Reading any of this
requires two controls that are not usually applied: a length-matched
content-free preamble, which is itself worth up to $15.7$ points, and
balanced accuracy on the boolean task, where raw accuracy rises with
density while the model converges on answering ``yes'' to $98$--$99\%$ of
pairs.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph} show that a large language model's
accuracy on graph-reasoning questions varies substantially with the choice
of text encoding when the graph and question are held fixed. We ask a
complementary question about the same benchmark: does prepending a short
\emph{primer} of derived, node-level facts to the graph encoding improve
the model's answers?

Four confounds complicate the interpretation of such an effect. First, a
primer may help because the model reads the answer from it rather than
reasoning over the graph (answer leakage). Second, a primer changes prompt
length, and context length and position alone affect retrieval and
reasoning accuracy \citep{liu2024lostinthemiddle,kamradt2023needle,
levy2024sametask}. Third, answer extraction can err in ways that correlate
with response style, which appears as a spurious condition effect
(Section~\ref{sec:extraction}). Fourth, a model near ceiling on a task ---
or one whose task has a majority class large enough to score well by itself
--- leaves no room for any primer effect to appear, or credits one that is
not there.

We ask what a primer does, and the headings of
Section~\ref{sec:results} are the questions in order. Which primer moves
which task? What does the primer change in the model's response --- how
much it writes, and which procedure it states? Where in the primer does it
read? Do primers combine? And which of the answers hold across model arms
and corpora? Section~\ref{sec:results-measurement} comes first and is
prerequisite rather than a result: at $n{=}40$ two of the six tasks have a
majority class that scores well by itself, one is bounded by the generation
budget, and a content-free preamble of matched length is already worth up
to $15.7$ points, so what counts as a primer effect has to be fixed before
any of it can be read.

Two contributions support the answers. The first is the four controls of
Figure~\ref{fig:design} applied jointly --- a graph-blind shortcut solver
refit at the evaluated graph size (Section~\ref{sec:shortcuts}), a
length-matched \texttt{filler}, a minimal-length \texttt{components}, and a
degree-preserving rewiring (Section~\ref{sec:rewiring}). The second is that
we read the generated text as well as the score, which is what lets us say
what a primer did rather than only how large its effect was.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

We adopt the GraphQA task definitions of \citet{fatemi2023talklikeagraph}
and manipulate graph size and edge density directly, so that difficulty is
an experimental variable rather than an incidental one.
\citet{wang2023nlgraph} report that LLMs are brittle to spurious
correlations in graph problems and propose \emph{Build-a-Graph} and
\emph{Algorithmic} prompting, the prompt-level interventions closest to a
primer; \citet{perozzi2024letyourgraph} instead learn a continuous graph
encoding. \citet{dziri2023faithandfate} argue that transformers solve
compositional tasks by linearised subgraph matching rather than systematic
reasoning, which raises the same question as a primer's benefit: does it
reflect reasoning or a shorter pattern to match?
\citet{geirhos2020shortcut} frame this as \emph{shortcut learning}; our
shortcut solver operationalises that concern for the primer channel.
Irrelevant context degrades reasoning \citep{shi2023distracted}, as does
input length well below the context limit \citep{levy2024sametask}; our
\texttt{filler} condition measures this cost directly. The \texttt{rwse}
condition adapts the random-walk structural encoding of
\citet{dwivedi2022graph}, rendered as sentences, and the rewiring follows
the double-edge-swap configuration model \citep{fosdick2018configuring}. We
distinguish Qwen3's native thinking mode, toggled through the chat
template, from chain-of-thought \emph{prompting}
\citep{wei2022chainofthought}; this paper tests only the former.

A primer is a preamble of factual sentences about a graph's structure,
prepended to the standard text encoding and the task question
(Figure~\ref{fig:design}; a complete example appears in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-prompt}). A single renderer produces all seven
conditions, so they share sentence framing and number formatting and differ
only in the facts stated. The exception is \texttt{filler}, which states no
fact and therefore uses its own frame (``Node~$N$ is simply present within
the graph G.''); it is a length control, not a content control.
\texttt{rwse} states each node's random-walk return probabilities after two
and three steps \citep{dwivedi2022graph}.

\subsection{Shortcut solvers}
\label{sec:shortcuts}

A primer's measured benefit is evidence of graph reasoning only if it
exceeds what a solver that never sees the graph can recover from the
primer text. Our shortcut solver combines sixteen exact rules, one
heuristic, eight fitted rules (fit and scored on disjoint graph sets) and an
exact enumeration bound for small graphs. Its accuracy on a (task,
condition) cell, the \emph{shortcut bar}, is a content-only lower bound: it
never overstates what the primer text alone permits.

Bars are fit at the evaluated graph size: $n{=}40$ Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi
graphs at the seven densities of the sweep, with disjoint $300$-graph fit
and test sets per density (Table~\ref{tab:shortcut}); the screened arms,
which answer the published $5$--$19$-node split, use bars fit on that split,
which differ substantially (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:shortcut-published}). A (task, condition) pair offers a
\emph{substitute route} when its bar exceeds the \texttt{none} bar by more
than $0.05$; every gain is either at most $0.011$ or at least $0.066$, so
any threshold between these values yields the same split. Only
\texttt{node\_degree} (under \texttt{rwse}, \texttt{degree}, \texttt{all})
and \texttt{edge\_count} (under \texttt{clustering}, \texttt{degree},
\texttt{all}) offer a route. \texttt{node\_count} and \texttt{cycle\_check}
have constant gold answers at $n{=}40$ ($40$, and ``yes'' at every density)
and are excluded.


\subsection{Rewiring: isolating structure from length}
\label{sec:rewiring}

\texttt{filler} controls length only approximately, and comparisons across
graph generators confound structure with class balance, so we use a
within-instance design. A double-edge swap replaces edges $(a,b),(c,d)$
with $(a,d),(c,b)$ \citep{fosdick2018configuring}, which preserves the
degree sequence, $n$, $m$, the \texttt{node\_degree} gold answer and the
prompt length while changing the triangle count that the
\texttt{clustering} primer reports. We generate \texttt{low},
\texttt{base} and \texttt{high} variants of each graph ($n{=}40$, mean
degree $12$, $200$ graphs per level; mean clustering $0.01$, $0.31$,
$0.73$) and evaluate \texttt{node\_degree} under \texttt{none},
\texttt{clustering} and \texttt{filler} with \texttt{qwen3-1.7b},
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think} and \texttt{qwen35-2b}. If the
\texttt{clustering} benefit derives from the stated values, it should vary
with triangle count. If instead the model reads mean clustering as a
density prior (in an Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graph $\bar{C}\approx p$), its
degree estimates should shift toward $\bar{C}\,(n-1)$: $0.5$, $12.0$ and
$28.7$ across levels, against a true mean of $12$.

\subsection{Answer extraction and measurement validity}
\label{sec:extraction}

Extraction errors can masquerade as condition effects, so answers are
extracted with task-specific patterns rather than a positional rule: on
\texttt{cycle\_check} a last-yes/no rule is not sufficient, because
\texttt{qwen3-4b} often answers correctly and then recites that a cycle has
``no repeated'' nodes, which a positional rule scores against one condition
more than another. A manual check of $50$ rows per arm confirmed that
\texttt{<think>} blocks never reach the extractor. Truncated pairs are
dropped; scoring them as incorrect changes the verdict on only three
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b} \texttt{cycle\_check} cells
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}).

\subsection{Significance testing}
\label{sec:significance}

Every comparison is paired on the same graph and question
\citep{dror2018hitchhikers} and tested with the exact McNemar test
\citep{mcnemar1947note}, Benjamini--Hochberg corrected
\citep{benjaminihochberg1995controlling} within each (arm, task)
family at $q{=}0.05$; a claim that a condition beats both controls also
requires significance under a global correction. For the effect--baseline
relation (Section~\ref{sec:results-supporting}), baseline and effect are
estimated from disjoint random halves of each cell's graphs
\citep{chernozhukov2018double}, and the route $\times$ baseline
interaction is bootstrapped over (arm, task, density) blocks, which share
one \texttt{none} sample. For null cells we simulate a
minimum detectable effect (MDE) at power $0.8$, $\alpha{=}0.05$.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks and arms.} The six GraphQA tasks
\citep{fatemi2023talklikeagraph} are those named in
Table~\ref{tab:main}. An \emph{arm} is a model checkpoint with a fixed
decoding configuration, so native thinking mode off and on gives two arms
of one checkpoint.

\paragraph{Why $n{=}40$.} On the published GraphQA split
\citep{fatemi2023talklikeagraph} --- $5$--$19$ nodes, five tasks --- eight
arms from two families score $0.97$--$1.00$ under \texttt{none} on four of
the five tasks (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ceiling}), leaving no headroom for
a primer to move. We therefore evaluate on graphs large and dense enough to
leave room in both directions.

\paragraph{Main sweep.} Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B \citep{qwen2025qwen3}
carry it, each with thinking off (\texttt{qwen3-1.7b}, \texttt{qwen3-4b})
and on (\texttt{-think}): two sizes, both modes of the same weights, and
the two sizes that retain headroom on five of six tasks at $n{=}40$. All
four arms answer one shared prompt set of Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs
\citep{erdosrenyi1959random} with $n{=}40$ at densities
$p\in\{.10,.20,.35,.50\}$, $100$ graphs per (density, task) cell, seven
conditions and six tasks, for $16{,}800$ prompts per arm, extended on
\texttt{node\_degree} and \texttt{edge\_existence} to
$p\in\{.65,.75,.85\}$ ($100$ graphs per cell, all four arms) so that the
density range reaches from near-ceiling to near-floor. Decoding is greedy
with a budget of $2{,}048$ new tokens for plain arms and $8{,}192$ for
thinking arms; Section~\ref{sec:results-supporting} reports a budget-matched
comparison. Every headline result below is measured on this sweep.

\paragraph{Supporting corpora.} Four further corpora each answer one
question the main sweep cannot, and each is used only for that question.
\emph{(i)} A dedicated \texttt{node\_degree} sweep with
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b} and \texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think} --- $400$ graphs per
density at all seven densities, with a \texttt{filler} arm, plus an
independent replication on $400$ newly seeded graphs per density at
$p\le.50$ --- supplies the power the main sweep lacks for the one
non-answer content effect (Section~\ref{sec:results-supporting}).
\emph{(ii)} A degree-preserving rewiring corpus
(Section~\ref{sec:rewiring}; \texttt{qwen3-1.7b},
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think} and Qwen3.5-2B \citep{qwen35},
\texttt{qwen35-2b}) tests whether that effect tracks the triangle count
the primer reports. \emph{(iii)} A fixed-mean-degree grid --- mean degree
$\approx 8$ or $\approx 16$ with $n\in\{20,40,80,160\}$ on
\texttt{node\_degree}, answered by \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} and
\texttt{qwen3-8b} --- separates answer size from graph size and tests the
same effect across four graph sizes (Section~\ref{sec:results-supporting}); it
carries no \texttt{filler} arm. \emph{(iv)} A $100$-graph probe of
\texttt{none} and \texttt{degree} on all six tasks, answered by Qwen3-0.6B
with and without thinking and by \texttt{qwen35-2b}, adds a third family
and a smaller size to the robustness estimate in Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:robust}.

\paragraph{Screened arms.} The eleven arms of the starting point and the
probe support the two checks of Section~\ref{sec:results-supporting}; their
shortcut bars are refit on their own corpus (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:shortcut-published}).

\paragraph{Metrics.} Exact match for every task; mean absolute error for
the integer tasks is in the Appendix. On \texttt{connected\_nodes} the
choice of metric decides whether an effect can appear at all: set-F1, the
metric that task is usually reported on, awards partial credit on a large
answer set and sits at $0.93$--$1.00$ in every cell, while exact match on
the same responses spans $0.48$--$0.92$. We report exact match throughout.
On \texttt{edge\_existence} we report balanced accuracy alongside accuracy,
because the share of node pairs that are joined rises from $10\%$ to $85\%$
across our density range (Section~\ref{sec:results-measurement}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{What these tasks and scores measure}
\label{sec:results-measurement}

A primer can only move accuracy where accuracy can move, and on two of the
six tasks the score moves for reasons that have nothing to do with the
primer. This section fixes what the numbers in the rest of the paper mean.

\paragraph{Accuracy on \texttt{edge\_existence} tracks the class prior.}
The share of node pairs that are joined rises with density, from $10\%$ at
$p{=}.10$ to $85\%$ at $p{=}.85$, so a constant answer earns a score that
changes with density. Under \texttt{none}, \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} scores
$89, 73, 66, 54, 68, 77, 86$ across the seven levels --- a decline and then
an apparent recovery. Balanced accuracy over the same responses runs
$93.9, 83.5, 75.4, 55.8, 52.9, 52.1, 53.3$: the recovery is entirely the
prior. Above $p{=}.50$ the model answers ``yes'' to $98$--$99\%$ of pairs,
and always saying yes scores $66$--$85\%$ there. The same split holds on
\texttt{qwen3-4b}, less severely (raw $96$ to $96$, balanced $97.8$ to
$89.4$). We read no gain above $p{=}.50$ as evidence about graph reading
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:balanced}).

\paragraph{Two tasks have no label distribution at all.} At $n{=}40$ every
one of the $400$ graphs contains a cycle and has $40$ nodes, so
\texttt{cycle\_check} and \texttt{node\_count} have a majority-class
baseline of $1.000$. \texttt{cycle\_check} is uninformative here.
\texttt{node\_count} is worse: \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} scores $0.315$, because
$68.5\%$ of its answers read the encoder's ``nodes $0,\dots,39$'' preamble
as the count. Almost any interposed text interrupts that off-by-one, which
is what the large primer gains on this task measure.

\paragraph{Truncation is an outcome, not a missing value.}
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b} reaches the output budget on $22.4\%$ of
\texttt{edge\_count} generations; the thinking arms reach it on $81.0\%$
and $62.3\%$. It decides scores elsewhere too: on \texttt{cycle\_check},
whose answer is ``yes'' for all $400$ graphs, \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} scores
$0.997$ on the generations that terminate and $0.923$ counting the rest as
wrong. Paired differences below drop a pair when either side truncated, the
least favourable convention for the primer; rates are in
Table~\ref{tab:hitcap}.

\paragraph{Difficulty is set by the answer, not the graph.} Holding mean
degree fixed while $n$ ranges over $\{20,40,80,160\}$, growing $n$
eightfold costs \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} $29$ points while doubling $\bar{d}$
costs $33$--$42$ at \emph{every} $n$ (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:degfixdeg}). The sharper version is available within the
main sweep, because one graph per (density, index) serves every task:
\texttt{node\_degree}, \texttt{connected\_nodes} and
\texttt{edge\_existence} ask about the \emph{same node of the same graph}
and differ only in whether the answer is a count, a set or a boolean. Under
\texttt{none} at $p{=}.50$, \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} scores $0.30$, $0.48$ and
$0.54$ on the three; at $p{=}.35$, $0.41$, $0.59$ and $0.66$. Counting a
neighbour set is harder than writing it out --- and \texttt{qwen3-4b}
reverses that ordering ($0.99$ against $0.79$ at $p{=}.50$), so the
required output operation is a difficulty driver in its own right, with
graph, density and target all controlled, but which operation is harder
depends on the model.

\paragraph{Added text is an active treatment on its own.} \texttt{filler}
is length-matched and states nothing about the graph, and it is not a null:
it significantly reduces accuracy on \texttt{connected\_nodes} and
\texttt{edge\_existence} in both plain arms. The cost is not a flat charge
for tokens. It differs by task by a factor of two (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:lengthcost}a); within a task it is not monotone in density,
running $-5$, $-20$, $-32$, $-6$, $-2$, $-1$, $-1$ from $p{=}.10$ to $.85$
on \texttt{qwen3-1.7b}/\texttt{edge\_existence}, largest in the middle and
gone where the task collapses toward a constant answer; and it changes sign
\emph{within} every task but the constant-gold \texttt{node\_count}, so
there is no per-token rate to quote and no transferring an estimate from
one task to another. Whether it tracks difficulty depends on the arm:
correlated with accuracy under \texttt{none} it gives $+0.55$ for
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think}, $-0.09$ for \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} and $-0.44$ for
\texttt{qwen3-4b}. Every difference below is therefore reported against
\texttt{filler} as well as against \texttt{none}.

On \texttt{edge\_existence} these are shifts in response bias rather than
in how the graph is read: the hit rate stays at $98$--$100\%$ under every
condition, so accuracy moves only through the false-alarm rate, which
\texttt{filler} raises and \texttt{clustering} lowers. With hits at
ceiling, sensitivity and criterion cannot be separated
\citep{macmillan2005detection,hautus1995corrections} (Appendix
Tables~\ref{tab:booleanbias} and~\ref{tab:sdt}).

\subsection{Which primer moves which task?}
\label{sec:results-effects}

\input{v3_main_table.tex}

Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:main} give the main sweep against
both baselines. Three things are visible in them that a single headline
would hide.

\paragraph{The sign is not a property of the primer.} \texttt{degree} is
worth $+26.5$ points against the length control on
\texttt{qwen3-4b}/\texttt{edge\_count} and $-6.0$ on the same arm's
\texttt{node\_degree}, where the primer states the requested number
outright. \texttt{clustering} is worth $+11.0$ on
\texttt{qwen3-4b}/\texttt{edge\_existence} and nothing on that arm's
\texttt{node\_degree}. Between arms, \texttt{degree} on
\texttt{node\_degree} runs $+8.0$ on \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} and $-6.0$ on
\texttt{qwen3-4b}. Effects reverse across task, across density and across
model size, so no primer is good or bad in itself
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}).

\paragraph{Effects are close to one-directional.} The paired design
decomposes each difference into items the primer fixed and items it broke,
and the two are rarely both large. At $p{=}.50$, \texttt{degree} on
\texttt{qwen3-4b}/\texttt{edge\_count} fixes $33$ items and breaks none;
\texttt{rwse} on that arm's \texttt{edge\_existence} breaks $23$ and fixes
none; \texttt{degree} on its \texttt{node\_degree} breaks $13$ and fixes
$1$. \texttt{clustering} is the exception, fixing $8$ and breaking $4$ on
\texttt{edge\_existence}, so its small net is a different kind of effect
from the others rather than a weaker version of the same one, and should
not be read as one.

\paragraph{The largest content effect we measure costs eight tokens.}
\texttt{components} on \texttt{connected\_nodes} gains $+9.2$ points over
\texttt{none} and $+21.8$ over \texttt{filler} for \texttt{qwen3-4b}
($n{=}400$, $p\approx10^{-4}$, both under the global correction;
Table~\ref{tab:main}). Every condition beats \texttt{filler} there,
because \texttt{filler} itself costs $12.5$ points; what distinguishes
\texttt{components} is that it also beats \texttt{none}. Since it adds $8$
tokens where \texttt{filler} adds $470$ (Figure~\ref{fig:design}), it is
the only effect we measure that is substantially free of length cost.

\paragraph{Carrying the right feature is neither necessary nor sufficient.}
Each primer reports one graph feature and each task needs one, so some
(primer, task) pairs are matched and the rest are not. The three largest
gains we measure are matched pairs --- \texttt{degree} on
\texttt{edge\_count} ($+26.0$ on \texttt{qwen3-4b}), \texttt{components} on
\texttt{connected\_nodes} ($+21.7$) and \texttt{clustering} on
\texttt{edge\_existence} ($+19.5$ on \texttt{qwen3-1.7b}). But mismatched
content is still worth $+8.1$ points on \texttt{qwen3-1.7b}, more than two
of the four matched pairs, and the fourth matched pair reverses with model
size. Relevance organises the largest effects without predicting the rest
(Appendix Table~\ref{tab:relevance}).

\subsection{What does the primer change in the response?}
\label{sec:results-behaviour}

Accuracy alone cannot say what a primer did. The generated text can, and it
shows the primer changing which procedure the model runs and how much work
it does, in directions that differ by task.

\paragraph{Before the primer, \texttt{edge\_count} is budget-bound.} Under
\texttt{none} the plain arms almost never answer
exactly --- \texttt{qwen3-4b} is exact on $8$ of $400$ with a median
relative error of $10.9\%$ and never truncates, \texttt{qwen3-1.7b} exact
on $5$ with $28.5\%$ over the $70\%$ of its generations that terminate ---
while the thinking arms are exact on $86\%$ and $98\%$ of the generations
that terminate and truncate on $82\%$ and $80\%$ of all of them. The task
is not beyond the plain arms' competence; without an explicit serial count
they substitute an estimate, and with one they run out of budget.

\paragraph{On \texttt{edge\_count} the primer replaces the method.} Under
\texttt{none} at $p{=}.50$, $7\%$ of \texttt{qwen3-1.7b}'s responses state
the handshake shortcut --- sum the degrees and divide by two --- and the
rest enumerate edges. Under \texttt{degree}, which lists all $40$ degrees,
$100\%$ state it. Truncation falls from $29\%$ to $20\%$ and the median
terminating response from $3{,}737$ to $818$ new tokens. The same switch
appears at every density, and at $p{=}.10$ runs $67\%$ to $99\%$. This is a
wholesale change of algorithm, not a nudge: the primer supplies an
arithmetic route around an enumeration the model cannot finish inside its
budget. \texttt{filler} induces the switch in $31\%$ of responses and
\texttt{all} in $40\%$, so stating the degrees is what carries it.

\paragraph{Where the primer hurts, reasoning collapses.} On
\texttt{qwen3-4b}/\texttt{node\_degree} at $p{=}.50$, where the
\texttt{degree} primer states the requested number verbatim, the median
response falls from $273$ new tokens under \texttt{none} to $34$, and
accuracy falls from $99$ to $87$ --- the primer breaks $13$ items and fixes
$1$. Neither cell truncates at all, so this is a choice and not a budget
limit. \texttt{filler} leaves the median at $266$ tokens and costs $2$
points, so it is the stated answer and not the added text that shortens the
response. The model abandons a reliable $273$-token count for an unreliable
$34$-token lookup.

\paragraph{The direction is task-specific.} On
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b}/\texttt{edge\_existence} the same primers make the
model do \emph{more}: $206$ tokens under \texttt{none} against $294$ under
\texttt{degree} and $352$ under \texttt{all}. On
\texttt{qwen3-4b}/\texttt{connected\_nodes}, \texttt{filler} alone cuts the
median from $233$ to $94$ while costing $12.5$ points --- a content-free
preamble that makes the model answer faster and worse. ``Primers make
responses longer'' and ``primers make responses shorter'' are both false as
general statements (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:behaviour}).

\paragraph{At high density the smaller model stops reasoning altogether.}
On \texttt{edge\_existence} beyond $p{=}.50$, \texttt{qwen3-1.7b}'s
responses shorten sharply and converge on the majority answer: it replies
``yes'' to $98$--$99\%$ of pairs and its balanced accuracy sits at chance
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:balanced}). Neither prompt length nor task
difficulty
triggers this. A ladder that raises $n$ at fixed mean degree makes the
prompt longer and the task harder without producing it, and the collapse
appears on the task whose answer space is a single token while the
enumeration tasks on the same graphs keep generating (Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:capitulation}). What the two-valued answer format permits
is a cheap guess that is right most of the time, and raw accuracy rewards
it.

\subsection{Where in the primer does the model read?}
\label{sec:results-retrieval}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{v3_fig_serial_position.pdf}
\caption{\texttt{node\_degree} accuracy by the queried node's line number
within the $40$-line primer, pooled over all seven densities
($n{=}700$ per condition). Primers that state the answer show the steepest
gradient; the controls, which contain no per-node value to retrieve, are
flatter but not flat.}
\label{fig:serial}
\end{figure}

If a primer is being retrieved from rather than reasoned over, accuracy
should depend on \emph{where} in the $40$ lines the queried node sits.
It does (Figure~\ref{fig:serial}). On \texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think} under
\texttt{degree}, accuracy runs
$91.2, 82.0, 86.2, 73.3$ across line-number quartiles, a first-to-last drop
of $17.9$ points ($p{=}2\times10^{-5}$); under \texttt{all} it is
$91.2, 79.1, 79.3, 69.6$, a drop of $21.6$ ($p{=}10^{-6}$). On
\texttt{qwen3-4b} under \texttt{degree} the drop is $9.5$ points
($p{=}0.014$) against $0.4$ under \texttt{none} ($p{=}0.79$).

The controls bound how much of this is retrieval. The primer's line order
is node-id order, so position is confounded with node identity, and a
gradient present without a primer would not be a retrieval effect. Under
\texttt{none} the gradient is $5.6$ points on
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think} ($p{=}0.10$) and $0.4$ on \texttt{qwen3-4b}
($p{=}0.79$) --- flat. But \texttt{filler} gives $12.1$ points on
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b-think} ($p{=}0.006$), and \texttt{filler} contains no
per-node value to retrieve. Position therefore matters most where a value
must be found, and the answer-stating primers show much the steepest
gradients, but a length-matched control on one arm produces a gradient of
its own, so the effect is not exclusively retrieval from the primer.

\subsection{Do primers combine?}
\label{sec:results-composition}

\input{v3_additivity_table.tex}

\texttt{all} renders \texttt{degree}, \texttt{clustering} and
\texttt{rwse} in one primer, so the parts and the bundle are both measured
on the same graphs. Bundling is lossy: across the $48$ cells where the
parts do anything, the bundle delivers a median $0.44$ of their sum
(Table~\ref{tab:additivity}). How lossy depends on how cells are weighted
--- the estimate runs from $0.29$ to $0.71$ --- so what the data support is
that the bundle falls well short of its parts, not a coefficient. The
shortfall is the same whether the parts help or hurt:
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b}/\texttt{edge\_existence} keeps $0.90$ of a $+12.8$ gain
and \texttt{qwen3-4b}/\texttt{edge\_existence} keeps $0.93$ of a $-10.8$
loss. That symmetry is what dilution of a useful sentence among irrelevant
ones would predict and not what interference would.
\texttt{edge\_count} is the exception: there the bundle keeps $0.15$ of a
$+24.5$ gain on \texttt{qwen3-4b} and moves against its parts on two
thinking arms. It is also the task whose gain depends on a single stated
procedure, which the bundle buries.

\subsection{What holds across arms and corpora?}
\label{sec:results-supporting}

\paragraph{Answer-stating primers track the baseline.} Where a primer
offers a substitute route to the answer, it gains on cells with low
accuracy under \texttt{none} and loses on cells with high accuracy, while
primers that offer no such route show no such relation. Estimated on
cross-fitted halves of each cell's graphs, so that baseline and effect do
not share sampling noise, the interaction is $-11.0$ points per unit of
baseline ($95\%$ CI $[-17.1, -5.7]$; Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:crossfit} and Table~\ref{tab:robust}). It holds within
\texttt{node\_degree} alone, which truncates on $0$--$1.6\%$ of
generations, so it is not an artifact of censoring, and it has the same
sign on the eleven screened arms. It is a contrast between floor and
ceiling rather than a graded dependence: restricted to baselines between
$0.10$ and $0.90$ it disappears.

\paragraph{One non-answer content effect replicates.} \texttt{clustering}
on \texttt{node\_degree} is the only effect of a primer that neither states
the answer nor merely pays for its length that we could replicate on a
dedicated corpus. Pooled over $p{=}.35$ and $.50$ it exceeds \texttt{none}
by $+5.9$ points ($95\%$ CI $[2.1, 9.8]$) and \texttt{filler} by a similar
margin, with \texttt{filler} and \texttt{none} indistinguishable there; an
independent-seed replication gives $+5.5$. On the fixed-degree grid of
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:degfixdeg} it gains $+6.2$ points ($n{=}3{,}191$;
$p{<}10^{-4}$), larger where the answer is larger. Neither mechanism we can
test explains it: under degree-preserving rewiring, mean clustering rises
from $0.01$ to $0.73$ while the benefit stays flat, and the signed error
shifts by less than $0.3$ where a density-prior account predicts up to $17$
(Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:rewiring} and Table~\ref{tab:prior}).

\paragraph{Thinking mode buys accuracy partly with budget.} Thinking brings
most cells to ceiling, and where it does not, truncation censors the task.
Rescored at the plain arms' $2{,}048$-token budget, which is exact under
greedy decoding, the 1.7B thinking arm still wins on
\texttt{edge\_existence} ($97.8$ against $70.5\%$) but loses on
\texttt{cycle\_check} ($38.0$ against $90.7\%$; Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:budget}). Many of the main-sweep nulls are uninformative:
\texttt{none} is already at floor or ceiling in $42\%$ of
\texttt{qwen3-1.7b}'s null cells and in all $33$ of
\texttt{qwen3-4b-think}'s. Where a gain could be detected, the median
detectable size is $5.5$ points for the 1.7B arms and $5.4$ for 4B.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A primer's effect on GraphQA accuracy is not a property of the
primer. It reverses across task, across density and across model size, it
is close to one-directional within a cell --- fixing many items and
breaking none, or the reverse --- and a content-free preamble of the same
length is itself worth up to $15.7$ points, so any effect measured against
\texttt{none} alone confounds content with length.

The generated text says what the primer did. A primer that lists
the degrees replaces edge enumeration with an arithmetic shortcut in every
response, cutting length and truncation with it; a primer that states the
requested number collapses the response from $273$ tokens to $34$ and costs
accuracy, breaking $13$ items to fix $1$. At high density the smaller model
stops reasoning on the two-valued task altogether and guesses the majority
class, which raw accuracy rewards and balanced accuracy does not.

What the effects depend on is partly legible and partly not.
Position inside the primer matters, by up to $21.6$ points, though a
length-matched control also slopes on one arm. Carrying the feature a task
needs organises the largest effects but predicts neither the rest nor the
sign. Bundling three primers recovers a median $0.44$ of their separate
worth, equally whether they help or hurt.

All claims are behavioural. Evaluations of prompt-level graph augmentations
should report a length-matched control, a graph-blind solver, a
majority-class baseline for every task, and enough of the generated text to
say which procedure changed.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The main sweep covers two model sizes from one family; the screened arms
extend the range but on a smaller corpus with fewer conditions and were not
pre-registered. All graphs are Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi, in which local
clustering is nearly uniform, so the \texttt{clustering} primer carries
little node-specific information; the rewiring varies triangle count within
this family but does not test other families. The length control matches
\texttt{clustering} to within $8\%$ in tokens but is much shorter than
\texttt{rwse} and \texttt{all}. Every prompt uses one encoding
(\texttt{incident}), one template and one primer position, with greedy
decoding. Several results rest on $400$ or fewer instances per cell; an MDE
is reported for every null result, but it does not replace a larger sample.

Three limits bear on the behavioural results specifically. The solution
strategy a response states is identified by regular expressions over free
text, hand-validated on a stratified sample, and a model may use a
procedure it does not narrate. Position within the primer is confounded
with node identity, because the primer's line order is node-id order; the
\texttt{none} and \texttt{filler} controls bound this, but \texttt{filler}
itself slopes on one arm, so we can say the gradient is steepest under
answer-stating primers and not that it is purely retrieval. The
response-length collapse is attributed to the answer format by eliminating
prompt length and task difficulty, not by manipulating the format directly.

Four further conditions would settle the main open questions:
\emph{shuffled-value} (a stricter placebo than \texttt{filler}),
\emph{corrupted} (to separate copying from computation), \emph{single-fact}
(to separate dilution from interference in the bundle) and a
\emph{reordered} primer (to break the position/identity confound above).

\section*{Ethics Statement}

This work evaluates open-weight language models on synthetically generated
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs. It involves no human subjects, no personal or
identifying data and no annotation labour. We see no direct risk of harm
from the findings; the principal cost is the GPU compute for approximately
$200{,}000$ generations.
